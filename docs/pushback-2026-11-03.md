# Pushback to the origin without a time to live

**Status:** candidate, measured 2026-11-03 in two rounds, the user
decides; roadmap D106. Spec references are to `protocol.md` by section
name. Round one is five commits on main `e4b20eb`, on the branch
`worktree-agent-pushback` at `f590c12` (code `44721a5`). Round two is
five commits on round one, on the branch `worktree-agent-pressure`,
measured with the code of `f8bea49` as it stood before its rebase on
main `9f665bf`. Both are measured against main `e4b20eb`,
release, paired by seed.

## Summary

The user's design, built in two rounds. Round one held a refused or
failed message on its neighbour and dropped the time to live. Round two
makes routing a view of the current state, adds a gate that lets a
relay hold one message per next hop, more only in order of count, picks
what to send from the whole queue whenever a hand-over may start, and
sizes the reply reserve to last the longest ban.

- **At the design rate**, in the city, S4 and with walkers, round two
  delivers what main delivers, with the median text no later (city
  7.5 → 5.6 s), and loses nothing to the time to live. The application
  is told to wait more often, 32 → 203 offers in the city's hour, at
  no cost a person would see.
- **Under overload the gate turns the overload into refusals that fill
  the air.** A refusal costs a full DATA frame and an ACK, and the gate
  mostly tells one ban, so a refused message comes back every 1.58 s.
  The city at three times the rate, where main and round one deliver
  97.5 and 97.4 % of what they take, falls to 67 %, with 4.2 times the airtime
  and a backlog growing by about 2 500 every ten minutes; S4 at three
  times delivers a quarter of main's texts. Without the gate, the rest
  of round two is better than main in S4 at three times: 82.8 % of the
  accepted against 78.3 %, more texts each ten minutes, less airtime.
  This is the headline.
- **The crossing traffic that gridlocked D29's one-place slot** does not
  lock the gate up: over seeds 1–10 the corridor is main's to the text,
  the gate never refusing there, the spread mesh delivers 892 of 900
  against 890, and the mesh burst 989 of 1 000 against 986.

## What was asked

The user's goal: overload must push back to the origin, where the
application is told a wait, instead of being taken by the network and
dropped by age. "It's OK if the message takes 20 minutes to reach the
destination, as long as every message accepted from the app is likely
to actually arrive." And of the neighbour a message waits for: "as long
as the neighbour is alive and hears us we should try." Then, for round
two: "let's optimize for origin back pressure, low latency, instead of
maximum throughput", and of the structure: "all messages would live in
a common data structure, and whenever we get a message we check if we
can put it there based on current state (known neighbours, airtime,
other packets that at this time would route to the same neighbour), and
whenever we get airtime we look at the structure to find the highest
priority message to send. Since the state changes from tick to tick I
don't want us to route things until we need to."

D104 (`ttl-2026-11-02.md`) found why the pushback of the time did not
reach the origin: a refused message chose its next hop at once, so in
the city, where a relay has some 61 live candidates, a refusal only
diverted it. D102 (`hold-2026-11-02.md`) had made a failed message wait
for the same neighbour, for three failures.

## Round one

Five rules: (1) any frame a neighbour addresses to this relay, an ACK
that takes, refuses or has seen, or a DATA, resets its failure count
and lifts its demotion; (2) a refused message waits the told ban and is
offered to the same neighbour again; (3) a refusal for want of room
tells no less than the queue waits before anything in it moves; (4) no
time to live: a message ends only by delivery, its search, the return
rules, the hop cap, the delivery queue's newer-message rule or a node
dying; (5) a held message, refused or failed, keeps its neighbour until
the neighbour is demoted, at three unanswered hand-overs in a row
(`tables::DEMOTE_AFTER_FAILURES`), has left the table or has had it.

Result, seed 1: up to three times the city's rate it delivered what
main delivered, losing nothing by age, at 3 to 12 % more airtime. At
four times in the city the backlog grew by about 1 000 messages every
ten minutes, delivered of accepted 95.1 → 84.8 %: the wait travelled
back only by filling 39-place rooms, so it stopped at the hop places of
some 160 hubs, and failed hand-overs outnumbered refused ones nine to
one. In S4 at three times the wait reached the origins, refusing 27 %
of texts against 11 %, but a quarter fewer texts arrived each ten
minutes. Its tables are in this note's history at `f590c12`; the
figures below repeat what round two is compared with.

## Round two: the rules

Five commits on round one, each with its spec change and tests, and a
sixth that only states in the spec what the first leaves open. Rules
not listed stay as in round one; (1) and (4) stay, (3) is re-expressed
per way, (2) and (5) are replaced by A.

**A. Routing is a view.** A queued message keeps no choice of
neighbour. Each time it is routed, its next hop is chosen from the
state as it is then: the live entries not in its tried set, ranked as
before (margin, not demoted, distance, ID). A ban no longer takes a
neighbour out; a banned next hop is waited for, and the message is
routed again when the ban ends (spec "Next hop"). So a neighbour that
refused or failed the message stays its next hop while it ranks first,
which it does until three hand-overs in a row go unanswered and demote
it (`tables::DEMOTE_AFTER_FAILURES`, kept with its derivation); any
answer to this relay keeps it from that. The neighbour table already
holds the failures in a row, the demotion and the ban, for every
message alike, so "never go round a neighbour that hears us" needs no
state per message. A neighbour that arrives, recovers or expires
changes the next answer and nothing else. A return is the same choice
with the parent its only candidate. Gone: round one's
`send_queue::State::Holding` and its `keeps`, `held_to`, `wait_for`,
and the older rule that set a neighbour aside for the rest of a
hand-over once its failure demoted it.

**B. The gate.** A relay takes a message to send on only if no message
queued here, deliveries aside, goes to the same next hop now, or the
new one's count is higher than every one of theirs; both are routed as
they would be from here now (spec "Send queue", "Receiving DATA"). It
holds for a DATA received, for a sleepy node's own message at its
anchor, and for the node's own application's message, which a refusal
at the gate gives back to the application with the wait, at least a
ban. A refusal at the gate tells the rest of that neighbour's ban, and
no less than the bucket needs to send what is queued for it with the
reply reserve left, rounded down in bans and outside the line. A
refusal for room tells, beside its place in line, the rest of the ban
on the packet's next hop: round one's rule 3, per way instead of per
queue.

- *Why one per next hop.* A neighbour at its cap takes one frame per
  ban, so a second message waiting here for it gains nothing; refused,
  it waits at the relay before, which fills in its turn, so the wait
  travels back one queue place at a time instead of 39.
- *What it costs, as designed.* Two messages for one next hop within a
  frame time, the second no further travelled, cost the second a ban at
  the relay before, although the neighbour might have taken both.
- *Why it does not lock up.* The hop places' argument holds: the
  message with the highest count anywhere, k, is sent with k + 1 or
  more; every message queued at its receiver holds k or less, so the
  gate lets it in, and place k + 1 is free there. Roadmap D29 measured a
  one-place send slot per relay that gridlocked under crossing traffic:
  the corridor 39–44 of 91–93 against 85–89, the mesh burst 68–89 of
  98–100 against 98–99, which is why the spec says nothing may wait on
  another node's occupancy. The gate differs in the two ways that
  matter: it bars one next hop, not the relay, so a message for any
  other way still enters, and a message with a higher count passes it,
  so the most travelled message always finds room.

**C. The send-time pick.** Whenever no hand-over is under way and the
radio is free, the relay routes every queued forward from the state as
it is; one whose next hop or parent is banned is not sendable until the
ban ends, and among the rest the queue's order holds: a delivery first,
then those that waited for a ban, the most travelled first, then
arrival. The gate keeps that order right, since the messages held for
one next hop are there only in order of count. The chosen hand-over
then waits for the bucket; a shorter frame does not pass it, or at the
cap the longer would never go. The cost is at most the queue's entries
times the neighbour table's per look, no allocation; only the earliest
ban end that held a message back is kept, for the wakeup. The entry
grows 272 → 280 B (a flag for having waited and a hint of where its
record is), the relay 168 920 → 171 272 B.

**D. The reply reserve lasts a longest ban under siege.** A relay at
its cap receives at most one DATA per full frame and ACK, 155.52 ms,
and sends a heartbeat at most every 5 s. Over the longest ban, the
bucket's whole refill, 61.0 s, the bucket refills exactly its depth,
6 s, while 13 heartbeats take 187.2 ms and the 391 refusals that fit in
the rest take 6631.4 ms: 818.6 ms short, ⌈818.6 / 16.96⌉ = 49 refusals,
845.4 ms with the heartbeat's 14.4 ms (spec "Duty cycle cap",
`budget::REPLIES`). Counting only the DATA gives 654 ms and 39
refusals; the heartbeats draw on the same bucket, so they count.
Before, 13 refusals, 235 ms, the replies one sender's retries cost.
With the reserve left in, a full bucket now takes 33 full frames, not
37. The demotion's three keeps its derivation, now stated from the 13
replies a sender's retries cost (`budget::RETRY_REPLIES`), since the
reserve no longer is that.

**E. Counters, simulator only.** DATA unanswered for want of reply
budget, and because another reply was owed (`Trace::Silent`); per
message in the log, the offers made (every hand-over that ended:
taken, refused, failed or seen), the distinct relay and neighbour pairs
offered, and flaps, offers at one relay to a neighbour, then another,
then the first again; and for each demotion the time to the neighbour's
next answer to the same relay (`Trace::Answered`), in bins.

**What the design leaves open.** Since nothing is set aside, a demoted
neighbour this relay hears but that never hears it is waited for while
it is the only candidate left; its heartbeats keep its entry, so the
message is neither sent on nor returned. The spec now lists this under
"Why the search finds a path", what breaks it. The runs below do not
single it out.

## How it was measured

Release builds of `bark-sim`: main `e4b20eb`, round one `44721a5`,
round two at `f8bea49`'s code, and, to tell the gate's part from the rest, round
two with the gate taken out (`Relay::gate` answering always that the
message may enter; a scratch build, not committed). Seed 1 in every
scenario; the corridor, the mesh burst and the spread mesh also seeds
1–10. One run per scenario and build, with the per-message log
(`--messages`), the JSON summary (`--json`) and, for the city at two to
four times, the per-relay series (`--series`).

Scenarios: the city, `dense-power.toml`, at the design rate and at two,
three and four times the texting rate; S4, `s4-capacity.toml`, at the
design rate and three times; the walkers, `dense-movers.toml`; the
ring, `ring.toml`; the mesh burst, `mesh-burst.toml`; and, where D29's
one-place slot gridlocked, the corridor, `corridor.toml`, and the
spread mesh, `mesh-spread.toml`.

The figures are of the settled hour, texts written from 2700 to 6300 s
(the ring and the burst from 390 s to their end), defined as in round
one:

- *texts*: written by the applications in the settled hour; an
  application with a text waiting writes no other meanwhile.
- *accepted*: taken by the origin's core and not given back for good;
  the simulator's application offers a refused text again until a told
  wait would take it past 600 s from its writing.
- *refused at origin*: never taken, or given back for good; *app told
  to wait*: offers that waited (the whole run).
- **delivered of accepted**: delivered by the end of the run; *within
  20 min*: of the accepted written with 20 minutes of run left.
- *latency*: of the delivered, from writing; *time in network*: from
  acceptance to delivery, loss or the end.
- *airtime per delivered text*: every frame of a delivered message;
  *airtime*: every frame of the run, heartbeats too.
- *texts delivered per 10 min*: deliveries in the settled window by
  when they arrived.
- *in flight every 600 s*: accepted messages in the network at each
  ten-minute mark of the whole run.
- *hand-overs taken / failed / refused*: summed over relays; *count-0*:
  a sleepy node's own message offered to its anchor; *told waits*:
  refusals by the wait told, in bans of 1.58 s.
- *relays ever at cap*: relays that once had less than the reply
  reserve and a full frame in the bucket. Round two's reserve is
  845 ms, not 235, so its count is not comparable with the others'.
- The new counters exist only in round two's builds.

In the series, "waiting for a ban" counted entries with a due time in
the future; round two keeps no such time for a ban, so that row is left
out for it.

## What it shows

**At the design rate round two costs a person nothing.** Delivery is
main's in the city, S4 and with walkers, the median text no later, and
nothing is lost by age. The application is told to wait four to six
times as often, 203 offers in the city's 18 234 texts.

**Under overload the gate breaks the network, and the measurement says
how.** A refusal is not free: the sender has put a full DATA frame on
the air to hear it, 138.6 ms, and the refuser answers with 17.0 ms. The
gate refuses whenever a message for the same next hop is queued ahead
with as high a count, which under load is most of the time, and what it
tells is the rest of that neighbour's ban or the bucket's time for the
message ahead, mostly less than a ban, so one ban: 1.78 of the 2.65
million refusals in the city at three times. The refused message is
offered again 1.58 s later and refused again. In the city at three
times that is 3.0 million refused DATA in the hour, the air 4.2 times
main's, failed hand-overs six times main's as the channels fill, and
relays at their cap by the thousand; the backlog grows by about 2 500
every ten minutes and a third of what was taken has not arrived at the
end. S4 at three times, one collision domain, does the same with
sleepy nodes' own messages at their anchors: 1.5 million refusals, the
origins refusing 62 % of texts, a quarter of main's texts delivered
each ten minutes. The bigger reply reserve (D) lets a relay afford
those refusals for a whole ban rather than fall silent, which is what
it was for, and here it pays for the storm.

**Without the gate, round two is round one or better.** In the city at
three times it delivers 96.8 % of the accepted against round one's
97.4 % and main's 97.5 %, at main's airtime, its backlog growing a
little faster than round one's; in S4 at three times it is better than
main on every count the user named: 82.8 % of the accepted delivered
against 78.3 %, 7.5 % refused at the origin against 11 %, 6 859 texts
each ten minutes against 6 121, 7 % less airtime. Routing as a view,
the send-time pick and the reserve sized to the longest ban do what
they were meant to, and cost nothing at the design rate.

**At four times in the city nothing built here holds the backlog.**
Without the gate round two is a little worse than round one: 80.2 % of
the accepted delivered against 84.8 %, 9 919 texts each ten minutes
against 10 441, the backlog growing by about 1 300 every ten minutes
against 1 000; main, with its time to live, delivers 95.1 % and 11 669.
The view and the pick do not bring back the faster service that kept
main's backlog nearly flat.

**The gate does not lock up under crossing traffic.** The corridor
never fills a queue, and the gate never refuses there; the mesh burst
over ten seeds delivers what main does, the gate refusing 218 times.
The progress argument holds; what fails is not progress but the air
that refusals take.

**What the counters say.** No relay in any round-two run fell silent
for want of reply budget: the reserve of 49 refusals is never spent.
Most demoted neighbours never answer the relay that demoted them again
within the run: walkers that have gone, and relays out of reach;
fewer than one in ten answer within the demotion's 390 s. Flaps are
rare at the design rate, a text in a hundred, and grow with the
storm.

**Origin pushback, as the user asked, against throughput.** The gate
does push the wait back to the origins: in the city at four times
round two refuses 15.6 % of texts at the origin against main's 0.8 %
and tells the application to wait a million times. But it does so by
spending the air the texts would have used, so what it takes it does
not deliver either: of the accepted 45 % arrive within the hour against
main's 95 %. Pushback that costs a DATA frame per refusal per ban does
not lower the load at the centre; it adds to it.

## What it does not show

- **Steady state.** One hour at four times the city's rate did not
  reach a steady state in any build; a four-hour run of main and round
  one is in progress elsewhere, none of round two.
- **Seeds.** One seed per scenario but the corridor and the meshes.
  The city's spread between seeds at twice the rate is about a point
  of delivery (roadmap D100); the differences under overload here are
  far larger.
- **The application.** The simulator's application gives up on a text
  told to wait past 600 s from its writing, and offers a refused one
  again as soon as it is told; a phone that waited longer between
  offers would put fewer refused frames on the air.
- **The gate's alternatives.** Only the gate as specified, and no gate,
  were run. A gate whose told wait grows with refusals in a row, or one
  that counts more than one message per next hop, was not built.
- **The simulator's newer radio model.** Main has since changed how the
  simulator receives two frames at once (roadmap D107); every run here
  used the model before it.
- **A one-way link** as the only candidate, which the design waits for
  without end, is not counted separately.

## Verdict

The user decides. This note's reading, judged against the user's
priority, origin pushback and latency over throughput, with throughput
reported plainly:

- **The design rate, the city, S4, the walkers:** round two is as good
  as main, a little faster in the city, and the application is told to
  wait a few hundred times an hour. No reason against it here.
- **Overload, with the gate as specified:** the goal is not reached.
  The wait does reach the origins, more than in round one, but the
  refusals that carry it fill the air, and of the accepted fewer
  arrive, later, than in main or round one, at two to four times the
  airtime. This is the measurement that shows the gate wrong as built:
  a per-next-hop limit enforced by refusing a full frame, told a wait
  of about one ban, repeats that refusal every ban for every message
  behind it.
- **Round two without the gate:** at three times the rate as good as
  round one in the city and better than main in S4; at four times in
  the city a little worse than round one, and like it far behind main,
  its backlog growing to the end of the hour. Of round two, the view,
  the pick, the reserve and the counters stand on their own up to three
  times the rate; the gate as built does not, and nothing here yet
  carries the city at four times.

What this note would try next, not built: a gate whose told wait grows
with refusals in a row from the same relay, as the silence ladder does,
so that a message refused at the gate is offered again a ban, two,
four later rather than every ban; or a gate that admits and holds the
second message rather than refusing it, and refuses only when the
queue for that next hop is longer than the neighbour takes in a told
wait.

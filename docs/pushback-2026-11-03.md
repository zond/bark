# Pushback to the origin without a time to live

**Status:** candidate, measured 2026-11-03, the user decides; roadmap
D106. Spec references are to `protocol.md` by section name. Built on
main `e4b20eb` (D105 in) as five commits on the branch
`worktree-agent-pushback`, `44721a5`; measured against `e4b20eb`,
paired by seed.

## Summary

Five rules, the user's design: any answer to this relay proves the
neighbour hears it; a refused message waits for the same neighbour; a
refusal tells the wait that blocks the queue; no time to live; one rule
for how long a held message keeps its neighbour.

- **Up to three times the design rate**, in the city, S4 and with
  walkers, the candidate delivers what the baseline delivers, of the
  accepted and within 20 minutes, and loses nothing by age where the
  baseline lost 11 to 199 texts to the time to live. It costs 3 to 12 %
  more airtime over the run, 6 to 38 % more failed hand-overs and
  longer told waits. The ring and the mesh burst gain.
- **The city at four times the rate**: the origins refuse 2.7 % instead
  of 0.8 %, but the network still takes 97 % of what the baseline takes
  and delivers 10 % less each ten minutes, so the backlog grows by
  about 1 000 messages every ten minutes to the end of the hour. Few
  are lost, 0.8 % of the accepted; 14 % are still in the network at the
  end; latency is four times as long and airtime per delivered text
  twice as much. The wait does not reach the origins: it stops at hop
  places of about 160 relays, and silence, not refusal, is what most
  hand-overs meet.
- **S4 at three times the rate**, where the bucket binds, the wait does
  reach the origins: they refuse 27 % instead of 11 %, and the accepted
  are lost 0.5 % instead of 7.6 %. But the network delivers a quarter
  fewer texts each ten minutes than the baseline, and its backlog grows
  faster.

## What was asked

The user's goal: overload must push back to the origin, where the
application is told a wait, instead of being taken by the network and
dropped by age. "It's OK if the message takes 20 minutes to reach the
destination, as long as every message accepted from the app is likely
to actually arrive." And of the neighbour a message waits for: "as long
as the neighbour is alive and hears us we should try."

D104 (`ttl-2026-11-02.md`) found why the pushback of the time did not
reach the origin: a refused message chose its next hop at once, so in
the city, where a relay has some 61 live candidates, a refusal only
diverted the message to another neighbour. The backlog grew in hop
places at the hubs and the edge relays, which take fresh work, never
saw it. D102 (`hold-2026-11-02.md`) had made a failed message wait for
the same neighbour, for three failures.

## The rules

Five commits, each with its spec change and tests.

**1. Any answer proves the neighbour hears us.** An ACK addressed to
this relay, whether it takes, refuses or has seen the packet, and a
DATA the neighbour addresses to this relay, reset the neighbour's
failure count, and so lift its demotion and the silence ladder (spec
"Neighbour table", "Bans"). Before, a refusal did not. A DATA to this
relay shows nearly what an ACK does: the neighbour chose this relay as
its next hop from its own table, so this relay's heartbeats reach it,
and it is awake and sending on this relay's channel. Heartbeats, and
frames to other nodes, still prove nothing about whether it hears us.

**2. A refused message waits for the same neighbour.** A refused
forward waits in its place for the ban it was told and is offered to
the same neighbour again; a ban grown meanwhile is waited for too (spec
"Bans", "Overload waits"). Kept, the message fills this relay's queue,
which refuses in its turn, and the wait travels back hop by hop.

**3. A refusal tells what blocks the queue.** With rule 2 a relay's
queue can be full of messages waiting out their next hops' bans while
its bucket is full; told only the bucket's refill, a sender would come
back in a second or two to be refused again. A refusal for want of
room now asks for the larger of the bucket's wait, with its place in
line, and, when every queued message waits, the rest of the wait of the
one due first (spec "Bans"). That rest stands outside the line: it
counts from when it was told and is rounded down at each refusal, so
passed back relay by relay it only shrinks and cannot grow round a ring
of relays that refuse each other. The ACK's six bits, 63 bans of
1.58 s, still hold it.

**4. No time to live.** A message ends only when it is delivered, when
its search runs out of neighbours, when a return's parent is demoted,
gone or answers seen, at the hop cap, when a newer message needs its
place in the delivery queue, or when a node holding it dies
(spec "Time to live", "Overload waits"). The DATA keeps its `time_left` byte, which
no node reads, so the format does not change. The delivery queue keeps
its own age rule. A sleepy node's own message unanswered by its anchor
keeps its doubling wait, capped at the delivery TTL, and is tried until
an anchor takes it (spec "A sleepy node's own message").

**5. One hold rule.** A held message, refused or failed, keeps its
neighbour until the neighbour is demoted, has left the table, or has
had the message, and then chooses its next hop afresh; a return is
dropped once its parent is demoted or gone (spec "Hand-over",
"Return to parent"). The demotion is the one test that the neighbour
no longer hears us, and it moves from two failed hand-overs in a row
to three (`tables::DEMOTE_AFTER_FAILURES`), now with no answer to this
relay between them. Why three: a receiver silent for want of reply
budget has its whole reply reserve back 2.4 s after it stops spending;
the failed message waits the ban, 1.58 s and then 3.16 s, so the third
offer comes 4.74 s after the first failure, when it can answer, while
the second may still find it short (spec "Neighbour table"). A
neighbour that does not answer holds a message at most 14.2 s, two bans
and three hand-overs; one that answers this relay between failures
holds it while its bans last, with no bound by count, since it hears
us (spec "Bounds"). The message's own failure count and
`MOVE_ON_FAILURES` (D102) are gone: with answers to this relay the only
resets, a neighbour that kept resetting the count is one that hears
this relay. The send-queue entry shrinks 288 → 272 B, 4.6 KiB a relay
(`core-interface.md` "Memory").

## How it was measured

Release builds of `bark-sim`: the baseline, main `e4b20eb`, and the
candidate, `44721a5`. Seed 1 in every scenario. One run per scenario
and build, with the per-message log (`--messages`), the JSON summary
(`--json`) and, for the city at two to four times, the per-relay
series (`--series`). A build of the branch before commit 5, with
D102's three failures for a failed message beside rule 2's hold for a
refused one, was started on the city at two to four times and not
measured to the end; nothing here is from it.

Scenarios:

- the city, `dense-power.toml`, 270 fixed relays and 20 000 people's
  nodes, relays while on USB, some walking (5 317 relays and 14 952
  sleepy nodes at four times), at the design
  rate, and at two, three and four times the texting rate (the same
  file with `per_leaf_per_hour` scaled);
- S4, `s4-capacity.toml`, one collision domain on 13 channels, at the
  design rate and at three times it, where the relays' buckets bind;
- the walkers, `dense-movers.toml`; the ring, `ring.toml`; the mesh
  burst, `mesh-burst.toml`.

The figures are of the settled hour, texts written from 2700 to
6300 s (the ring and the burst from 390 s to their end):

- *texts*: written by the applications in the settled hour. An
  application with a text waiting for its core writes no other
  meanwhile, so the count falls where the origins refuse.
- *accepted*: taken by the origin's core and not given back for good.
  The simulator's application offers a refused text again until a told
  wait would take it past 600 s from when it was written, then gives up.
- *refused at origin*: never taken, or given back for good.
- *app told to wait*: texts whose origin took them only after a wait
  (the whole run).
- *delivered of all written*: delivered, of the texts, the JSON's
  figure; the same over the whole run.
- **delivered of accepted**: delivered by the end of the run, of the
  accepted. The figure the user's goal is about.
- *within 20 min*: delivered within 20 minutes of being written, of the
  accepted written with 20 minutes of run left.
- *latency*: of the delivered, from writing.
- *airtime per delivered text*: every frame of a delivered message, all
  hops and attempts; *airtime*: every frame of the run, heartbeats too.
- *lost*: accepted and dropped anywhere but the origin, by reason.
- *in flight at end*: accepted and neither delivered nor lost when the
  run ends; *every 600 s*: all accepted messages in the network at each
  ten-minute mark of the whole run.
- *time in network*: from acceptance to delivery, loss or the end.
- *hand-overs taken / failed / refused*: summed over relays; *count-0*:
  a sleepy node's own message offered to its anchor.
- *told waits*: refusals by the wait told, in bans of 1.58 s.
- *relays ever at cap*: relays that once had less than the reply
  reserve and a full frame in the bucket.

Moving on at the first failure, the neighbour already demoted by other
messages' failures, is 61 841 of the candidate's 786 514 at four times;
after three, its own, 702 305.

What this can say. In the city at four times 786 514 hand-overs ended
with the message moving on, a quarter of them leaving the receiver
holding it, two copies searching; the baseline moved on 300 292 times.
The holds past three failed offers exist only in the candidate: a
neighbour that answered this relay between failures kept the message,
and 3 871 times took it at the fourth offer or later, against 9 925
times that it was left after all.

What it cannot say. The message log records no move, so a move cannot
be followed to the message's fate: how many moved messages were then
delivered by another way is not in the data. Only a bound: of the
accepted, 0.8 % were lost and 14 % were still in the network at the
end, whatever their moves. Nor does the simulator record when a
demotion lifts or when a demoted neighbour next answers, so whether the
silent neighbours answered again shortly after cannot be read either.
Both need a counter: a move's message tagged in the log, and for each
demotion the time to the neighbour's next answer to this relay.

## What it shows

**Up to three times the design rate the user's goal holds**, in this
seed: nothing accepted is dropped by age, delivery of the accepted and
within 20 minutes are the baseline's, and the origins are told a wait a
little more often. The costs are small and real: 3 to 12 % more airtime
over the run, 6 to 38 % more failed hand-overs, half again as many
moves and splits in the city, and two to five times the relays ever at
the cap. In the city, fewer demotions with more failures fit rule 1, a
neighbour that answers some frames staying a first choice; the cause
was not traced further.

**At four times in the city it does not hold.** The network takes 97 %
of what the baseline takes and delivers 89 % of what the baseline
delivers each ten minutes, so the backlog grows by about 1 000
messages every ten minutes to the end, and each ten minutes' texts do
worse than the last. What kept the baseline's backlog nearly flat was
not mainly the time to live, 325 texts in the hour, but a faster
service: half the failed hand-overs, half the airtime per text, and 15
hops to the median text against 20. The wait does not reach the origins: the
backlog sits in hop places at the 160 relays with 19 or more queued,
which hold what reaches them whatever their bucket (spec "Send queue"),
while fresh work is still taken at relays with 0 to 5 queued. Fresh
work refused at relays with 19 to 40 queued, 2 442 → 18 452, shows the
wait reaching some admitting relays, not most. Hand-overs failed
outnumber those refused nine to one: what slows the centre is silence
on a busy channel, and silence sets a ban, not a told wait: it reaches
the senders behind only through rule 3, once every message in a queue
waits. A message kept
for a neighbour that answers this relay between failures is offered to
it again and again, the longest such hold 45 minutes.

**In S4 at three times, where the bucket binds, the wait does reach
the origins**: they refuse 27 % and the accepted are nearly never lost.
But the network delivers a quarter fewer texts each ten minutes, its
backlog grows faster than the baseline's, and of the texts people
wrote in the hour 53 % arrived against 70 %. Its air carries 16 % more
with fewer texts delivered: 124 000 more refused hand-overs and 76 000
more refused offers of fresh work, each a frame and an ACK that move
nothing. The first groups of texts gain, the expired ones arriving;
the later lose.

## What it does not show

- **Steady state at four times the rate.** One hour at four times did
  not reach a steady state in either build; a four-hour run of both is
  in progress.
- **Seeds.** One seed per scenario. The city's spread between seeds at
  twice the rate is about a point of delivery (roadmap D100); the
  differences at four times and in S4 at three times are larger.
- **The application.** The simulator's application gives up on a text
  told to wait past 600 s from its writing; "refused at origin" and
  "accepted" move with that patience.
- **The causes.** The rise in failed hand-overs at the design rate, and
  the loss of throughput in S4 at three times, are measured and not
  traced to a rule; the five rules were measured together.

## Verdict

The user decides. This note's reading, rate by rate:

- **Design rate to three times, city, S4 and walkers:** the goal is
  reached; the cost is airtime and failed hand-overs of a few to a few
  tens of percent.
- **The city at four times:** not reached. Little is lost, but a growing
  part of what is accepted is still in the network an hour later and

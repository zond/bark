# Pushback to the origin without a time to live

**Status:** measured 2026-11-03 to 05 in seven rounds; round five, on
the base of rounds one and two, adopted 2026-11-04 by the user, the
counts of places of rounds two to four rejected; roadmap D106, D109;
round six's build C adopted 2026-11-05 as D110; round seven measured
2026-11-05 and adopted as D115. Spec references are to `protocol.md` by section
name. Round one is five commits on main `e4b20eb`, on the branch
`worktree-agent-pushback` at `f590c12` (code `44721a5`). Round two is
five commits on round one, on the branch `worktree-agent-pressure`,
measured with the code of E (now `034a6a4`) as it stood before its
rebases. Both are measured against main `e4b20eb`, release, paired by
seed. Round three is three commits on round two, built and measured on
main `9f665bf` with main's newer radio model (roadmap D107), and since
rebased on main `b90d401`; see "Round three: how it was measured".

## Summary

The user's design, built in two rounds. Round one held a refused or
failed message on its neighbour and dropped the time to live. Round two
makes routing a view of the current state, adds a gate that lets a
relay hold one message per next hop, more only in order of count, picks
what to send from the whole queue whenever a hand-over may start, and
sizes the reply reserve to last the longest ban.

- **At the design rate**, in the city, S4 and with walkers, round two
  delivers what main delivers, with the median text no later (city
  7.5 → 5.6 s), and loses nothing to the time to live. The application
  is told to wait more often, 32 → 203 offers in the city's hour, at
  no cost a person would see.
- **Under overload the gate turns the overload into refusals that fill
  the air.** A refusal costs a full DATA frame and an ACK, and the gate
  mostly tells one ban, so a refused message comes back every 1.58 s.
  The city at three times the rate, where main and round one deliver
  97.5 and 97.4 % of what they take, falls to 67 %, with 4.2 times the airtime
  and a backlog growing by about 2 500 every ten minutes; S4 at three
  times delivers a quarter of main's texts. Without the gate, the rest
  of round two is better than main in S4 at three times: 82.8 % of the
  accepted against 78.3 %, more texts each ten minutes, less airtime.
  This is the headline.
- **The crossing traffic that gridlocked D29's one-place slot** does not
  lock the gate up: over seeds 1–10 the corridor is main's to the text,
  the gate never refusing there, the spread mesh delivers 892 of 900
  against 890, and the mesh burst 989 of 1 000 against 986.
- **Round three** keeps the gate and tells a refused sender a place in
  the way's line, a service time apart, measured per neighbour, and ends
  a message's wait for a neighbour at its demotion. It removes most of
  round two's storm: in the city at three times 95.3 % of the accepted
  arrive (round two 67.4, main 97.5, round two without the gate 96.8),
  in S4 at three times 81.7 % (42.3, 78.3, 82.8). At the design rate it
  is main's. In the city at four times it still fails: 63.0 % against
  main's 95.1 % and the gateless 80.2 %. It does not beat round two
  without the gate anywhere under overload but in S4's within-20-minute
  share.

- **Round four** gives the gate 1 to 16 places per next hop: from three
  places on it is no count at all, and at one or two it refuses millions
  of frames. Rejected.
- **Round five** gates on time: a relay refuses a message whose wait for
  its way would pass 30 s, and the ACK can tell waits of minutes. At the
  design's rate it refuses nothing; at capacity it does almost nothing,
  queues being short; far past it, the district at 64 times, it delivers
  the most of every build with the least air. Adopted.

## What was asked

The user's goal: overload must push back to the origin, where the
application is told a wait, instead of being taken by the network and
dropped by age. "It's OK if the message takes 20 minutes to reach the
destination, as long as every message accepted from the app is likely
to actually arrive." And of the neighbour a message waits for: "as long
as the neighbour is alive and hears us we should try." Then, for round
two: "let's optimize for origin back pressure, low latency, instead of
maximum throughput", and of the structure: "all messages would live in
a common data structure, and whenever we get a message we check if we
can put it there based on current state (known neighbours, airtime,
other packets that at this time would route to the same neighbour), and
whenever we get airtime we look at the structure to find the highest
priority message to send. Since the state changes from tick to tick I
don't want us to route things until we need to."

D104 (`ttl-2026-11-02.md`) found why the pushback of the time did not
reach the origin: a refused message chose its next hop at once, so in
the city, where a relay has some 61 live candidates, a refusal only
diverted it. D102 (`hold-2026-11-02.md`) had made a failed message wait
for the same neighbour, for three failures.

## Round one

Five rules: (1) any frame a neighbour addresses to this relay, an ACK
that takes, refuses or has seen, or a DATA, resets its failure count
and lifts its demotion; (2) a refused message waits the told ban and is
offered to the same neighbour again; (3) a refusal for want of room
tells no less than the queue waits before anything in it moves; (4) no
time to live: a message ends only by delivery, its search, the return
rules, the hop cap, the delivery queue's newer-message rule or a node
dying; (5) a held message, refused or failed, keeps its neighbour until
the neighbour is demoted, at three unanswered hand-overs in a row
(`tables::DEMOTE_AFTER_FAILURES`), has left the table or has had it.

Result, seed 1: up to three times the city's rate it delivered what
main delivered, losing nothing by age, at 3 to 12 % more airtime. At
four times in the city the backlog grew by about 1 000 messages every
ten minutes, delivered of accepted 95.1 → 84.8 %: the wait travelled
back only by filling 39-place rooms, so it stopped at the hop places of
some 160 hubs, and failed hand-overs outnumbered refused ones nine to
one. In S4 at three times the wait reached the origins, refusing 27 %
of texts against 11 %, but a quarter fewer texts arrived each ten
minutes. Its tables are in this note's history at `f590c12`; the
figures below repeat what round two is compared with.

## Round two: the rules

Five commits on round one, each with its spec change and tests, and a
sixth that only states in the spec what the first leaves open. Rules
not listed stay as in round one; (1) and (4) stay, (3) is re-expressed
per way, (2) and (5) are replaced by A.

**A. Routing is a view.** A queued message keeps no choice of
neighbour. Each time it is routed, its next hop is chosen from the
state as it is then: the live entries not in its tried set, ranked as
before (margin, not demoted, distance, ID). A ban no longer takes a
neighbour out; a banned next hop is waited for, and the message is
routed again when the ban ends (spec "Next hop"). So a neighbour that
refused or failed the message stays its next hop while it ranks first,
which it does until three hand-overs in a row go unanswered and demote
it (`tables::DEMOTE_AFTER_FAILURES`, kept with its derivation); any
answer to this relay keeps it from that. The neighbour table already
holds the failures in a row, the demotion and the ban, for every
message alike, so "never go round a neighbour that hears us" needs no
state per message. A neighbour that arrives, recovers or expires
changes the next answer and nothing else. A return is the same choice
with the parent its only candidate. Gone: round one's
`send_queue::State::Holding` and its `keeps`, `held_to`, `wait_for`,
and the older rule that set a neighbour aside for the rest of a
hand-over once its failure demoted it.

**B. The gate.** A relay takes a message to send on only if no message
queued here, deliveries aside, goes to the same next hop now, or the
new one's count is higher than every one of theirs; both are routed as
they would be from here now (spec "Send queue", "Receiving DATA"). It
holds for a DATA received, for a sleepy node's own message at its
anchor, and for the node's own application's message, which a refusal
at the gate gives back to the application with the wait, at least a
ban. A refusal at the gate tells the rest of that neighbour's ban, and
no less than the bucket needs to send what is queued for it with the
reply reserve left, rounded down in bans and outside the line. A
refusal for room tells, beside its place in line, the rest of the ban
on the packet's next hop: round one's rule 3, per way instead of per
queue.

- *Why one per next hop.* A neighbour at its cap takes one frame per
  ban, so a second message waiting here for it gains nothing; refused,
  it waits at the relay before, which fills in its turn, so the wait
  travels back one queue place at a time instead of 39.
- *What it costs, as designed.* Two messages for one next hop within a
  frame time, the second no further travelled, cost the second a ban at
  the relay before, although the neighbour might have taken both.
- *Why it does not lock up.* The hop places' argument holds: the
  message with the highest count anywhere, k, is sent with k + 1 or
  more; every message queued at its receiver holds k or less, so the
  gate lets it in, and place k + 1 is free there. Roadmap D29 measured a
  one-place send slot per relay that gridlocked under crossing traffic:
  the corridor 39–44 of 91–93 against 85–89, the mesh burst 68–89 of
  98–100 against 98–99, which is why the spec says nothing may wait on
  another node's occupancy. The gate differs in the two ways that
  matter: it bars one next hop, not the relay, so a message for any
  other way still enters, and a message with a higher count passes it,
  so the most travelled message always finds room.

**C. The send-time pick.** Whenever no hand-over is under way and the
radio is free, the relay routes every queued forward from the state as
it is; one whose next hop or parent is banned is not sendable until the
ban ends, and among the rest the queue's order holds: a delivery first,
then those that waited for a ban, the most travelled first, then
arrival. The gate keeps that order right, since the messages held for
one next hop are there only in order of count. The chosen hand-over
then waits for the bucket; a shorter frame does not pass it, or at the
cap the longer would never go. The cost is at most the queue's entries
times the neighbour table's per look, no allocation; only the earliest
ban end that held a message back is kept, for the wakeup. The entry
grows 272 → 280 B (a flag for having waited and a hint of where its
record is), the relay 168 920 → 171 272 B.

**D. The reply reserve lasts a longest ban under siege.** A relay at
its cap receives at most one DATA per full frame and ACK, 155.52 ms,
and sends a heartbeat at most every 5 s. Over the longest ban, the
bucket's whole refill, 61.0 s, the bucket refills exactly its depth,
6 s, while 13 heartbeats take 187.2 ms and the 391 refusals that fit in
the rest take 6631.4 ms: 818.6 ms short, ⌈818.6 / 16.96⌉ = 49 refusals,
845.4 ms with the heartbeat's 14.4 ms (spec "Duty cycle cap",
`budget::REPLIES`). Counting only the DATA gives 654 ms and 39
refusals; the heartbeats draw on the same bucket, so they count.
Before, 13 refusals, 235 ms, the replies one sender's retries cost.
With the reserve left in, a full bucket now takes 33 full frames, not
37. The demotion's three keeps its derivation, now stated from the 13
replies a sender's retries cost (`budget::RETRY_REPLIES`), since the
reserve no longer is that.

**E. Counters, simulator only.** DATA unanswered for want of reply
budget, and because another reply was owed (`Trace::Silent`); per
message in the log, the offers made (every hand-over that ended:
taken, refused, failed or seen), the distinct relay and neighbour pairs
offered, and flaps, offers at one relay to a neighbour, then another,
then the first again; and for each demotion the time to the neighbour's
next answer to the same relay (`Trace::Answered`), in bins.

**What the design leaves open.** Since nothing is set aside, a demoted
neighbour this relay hears but that never hears it is waited for while
it is the only candidate left; its heartbeats keep its entry, so the
message is neither sent on nor returned. Round two's spec listed this
under "Why the search finds a path"; round three's H ends it.

## How it was measured

Release builds of `bark-sim`: main `e4b20eb`, round one `44721a5`,
round two at `034a6a4`'s code, and, to tell the gate's part from the rest, round
two with the gate taken out (`Relay::gate` answering always that the
message may enter; a scratch build, not committed). Seed 1 in every
scenario; the corridor, the mesh burst and the spread mesh also seeds
1–10. One run per scenario and build, with the per-message log
(`--messages`), the JSON summary (`--json`) and, for the city at two to
four times, the per-relay series (`--series`).

Scenarios: the city, `dense-power.toml`, at the design rate and at two,
three and four times the texting rate; S4, `s4-capacity.toml`, at the
design rate and three times; the walkers, `dense-movers.toml`; the
ring, `ring.toml`; the mesh burst, `mesh-burst.toml`; and, where D29's
one-place slot gridlocked, the corridor, `corridor.toml`, and the
spread mesh, `mesh-spread.toml`.

The figures are of the settled hour, texts written from 2700 to 6300 s
(the ring and the burst from 390 s to their end), defined as in round
one:

- *texts*: written by the applications in the settled hour; an
  application with a text waiting writes no other meanwhile.
- *accepted*: taken by the origin's core and not given back for good;
  the simulator's application offers a refused text again until a told
  wait would take it past 600 s from its writing.
- *refused at origin*: never taken, or given back for good; *app told
  to wait*: offers that waited (the whole run).
- **delivered of accepted**: delivered by the end of the run; *within
  20 min*: of the accepted written with 20 minutes of run left.
- *latency*: of the delivered, from writing; *time in network*: from
  acceptance to delivery, loss or the end.
- *airtime per delivered text*: every frame of a delivered message;
  *airtime*: every frame of the run, heartbeats too.
- *texts delivered per 10 min*: deliveries in the settled window by
  when they arrived.
- *in flight every 600 s*: accepted messages in the network at each
  ten-minute mark of the whole run.
- *hand-overs taken / failed / refused*: summed over relays; *count-0*:
  a sleepy node's own message offered to its anchor; *told waits*:
  refusals by the wait told, in bans of 1.58 s.
- *relays ever at cap*: relays that once had less than the reply
  reserve and a full frame in the bucket. Round two's reserve is
  845 ms, not 235, so its count is not comparable with the others'.
- The new counters exist only in round two's builds.

In the series, "waiting for a ban" counted entries with a due time in
the future; round two keeps no such time for a ban, so that row is left
out for it.

## What it shows

**At the design rate round two costs a person nothing.** Delivery is
main's in the city, S4 and with walkers, the median text no later, and
nothing is lost by age. The application is told to wait four to six
times as often, 203 offers in the city's 18 234 texts.

**Under overload the gate breaks the network, and the measurement says
how.** A refusal is not free: the sender has put a full DATA frame on
the air to hear it, 138.6 ms, and the refuser answers with 17.0 ms. The
gate refuses whenever a message for the same next hop is queued ahead
with as high a count, which under load is most of the time, and what it
tells is the rest of that neighbour's ban or the bucket's time for the
message ahead, mostly less than a ban, so one ban: 1.78 of the 2.65
million refusals in the city at three times. The refused message is
offered again 1.58 s later and refused again. In the city at three
times that is 3.0 million refused DATA in the hour, the air 4.2 times
main's, failed hand-overs six times main's as the channels fill, and
relays at their cap by the thousand; the backlog grows by about 2 500
every ten minutes and a third of what was taken has not arrived at the
end. S4 at three times, one collision domain, does the same with
sleepy nodes' own messages at their anchors: 1.5 million refusals, the
origins refusing 62 % of texts, a quarter of main's texts delivered
each ten minutes. The bigger reply reserve (D) lets a relay afford
those refusals for a whole ban rather than fall silent, which is what
it was for, and here it pays for the storm.

**Without the gate, round two is round one or better.** In the city at
three times it delivers 96.8 % of the accepted against round one's
97.4 % and main's 97.5 %, at main's airtime, its backlog growing a
little faster than round one's; in S4 at three times it is better than
main on every count the user named: 82.8 % of the accepted delivered
against 78.3 %, 7.5 % refused at the origin against 11 %, 6 859 texts
each ten minutes against 6 121, 7 % less airtime. Routing as a view,
the send-time pick and the reserve sized to the longest ban do what
they were meant to, and cost nothing at the design rate.

**At four times in the city nothing built here holds the backlog.**
Without the gate round two is a little worse than round one: 80.2 % of
the accepted delivered against 84.8 %, 9 919 texts each ten minutes
against 10 441, the backlog growing by about 1 300 every ten minutes
against 1 000; main, with its time to live, delivers 95.1 % and 11 669.
The view and the pick do not bring back the faster service that kept
main's backlog nearly flat.

**The gate does not lock up under crossing traffic.** The corridor
never fills a queue, and the gate never refuses there; the mesh burst
over ten seeds delivers what main does, the gate refusing 218 times.
The progress argument holds; what fails is not progress but the air
that refusals take.

**What the counters say.** No relay in any round-two run fell silent
for want of reply budget: the reserve of 49 refusals is never spent.
Most demoted neighbours never answer the relay that demoted them again
within the run: walkers that have gone, and relays out of reach;
fewer than one in ten answer within the demotion's 390 s. Flaps are
rare at the design rate, a text in a hundred, and grow with the
storm.

**Origin pushback, as the user asked, against throughput.** The gate
does push the wait back to the origins: in the city at four times
round two refuses 15.6 % of texts at the origin against main's 0.8 %
and tells the application to wait a million times. But it does so by
spending the air the texts would have used, so what it takes it does
not deliver either: of the accepted 45 % arrive within the hour against
main's 95 %. Pushback that costs a DATA frame per refusal per ban does
not lower the load at the centre; it adds to it.

## What it does not show

- **Steady state.** One hour at four times the city's rate did not
  reach a steady state in any build; a four-hour run of main and round
  one is in progress elsewhere, none of round two.
- **Seeds.** One seed per scenario but the corridor and the meshes.
  The city's spread between seeds at twice the rate is about a point
  of delivery (roadmap D100); the differences under overload here are
  far larger.
- **The application.** The simulator's application gives up on a text
  told to wait past 600 s from its writing, and offers a refused one
  again as soon as it is told; a phone that waited longer between
  offers would put fewer refused frames on the air.
- **The gate's alternatives.** Only the gate as specified, and no gate,
  were run. A gate whose told wait grows with refusals in a row, or one
  that counts more than one message per next hop, was not built.
- **The simulator's newer radio model.** Main has since changed how the
  simulator receives two frames at once (roadmap D107); every run here
  used the model before it.
- **A one-way link** as the only candidate, which the design waits for
  without end, is not counted separately.

## Verdict

The user decides. This note's reading, judged against the user's
priority, origin pushback and latency over throughput, with throughput
reported plainly:

- **The design rate, the city, S4, the walkers:** round two is as good
  as main, a little faster in the city, and the application is told to
  wait a few hundred times an hour. No reason against it here.
- **Overload, with the gate as specified:** the goal is not reached.
  The wait does reach the origins, more than in round one, but the
  refusals that carry it fill the air, and of the accepted fewer
  arrive, later, than in main or round one, at two to four times the
  airtime. This is the measurement that shows the gate wrong as built:
  a per-next-hop limit enforced by refusing a full frame, told a wait
  of about one ban, repeats that refusal every ban for every message
  behind it.
- **Round two without the gate:** at three times the rate as good as
  round one in the city and better than main in S4; at four times in
  the city a little worse than round one, and like it far behind main,
  its backlog growing to the end of the hour. Of round two, the view,
  the pick, the reserve and the counters stand on their own up to three
  times the rate; the gate as built does not, and nothing here yet
  carries the city at four times.

What this note would try next, not built: a gate whose told wait grows
with refusals in a row from the same relay, as the silence ladder does,
so that a message refused at the gate is offered again a ban, two,
four later rather than every ban; or a gate that admits and holds the
second message rather than refusing it, and refuses only when the
queue for that next hop is longer than the neighbour takes in a told
wait.

## Round three: the rules

What the user asked, 2026-11-03, after round two's measurements: "We
need to do better model prediction of how long it will take to tx the
message. How many of my handovers to the next hop succeed, how often can
I try, how often is the slot empty. If relay A receives 10 messages with
next hop B in the time it takes to effectively send 1 message to B, they
should get incrementally increasing wait times, not 1 ban each."

Three commits on round two, each with its spec change and tests. A, C,
D and E of round two stay, and so does the gate: one message per next
hop, a higher count cutting in. What changes is what a refusal for want
of the way on tells, and how long a message waits for a neighbour that
does not hear this relay.

**F. The service time per neighbour.** Each neighbour entry keeps how
long this relay takes to get one message through to it: a running
average of the time from when a message began to wait for it here, or
from the neighbour's last take if that is later, to the ACK that takes
the message (spec "Neighbour table"). A message begins to wait for a
neighbour when it is queued, or when routing first sends it there
instead of elsewhere. The time folds in unanswered attempts and the
bans after them, this relay's bucket, and its turn behind hand-overs to
other neighbours. It leaves out time with no message waiting, which
says nothing about how fast the neighbour takes, and the waits the
neighbour's refusals told, which are the neighbour's own measure passed
on. Before any take it is one ban; it never exceeds 63 bans.

- *The one new constant*, `SERVICE_TAKES` = 39: each take counts one
  part in 39. The average follows the neighbour's state, which its
  bucket sets; at its cap a neighbour takes one full frame a ban, and
  its bucket refills from empty in 61.0 s, so 39 takes
  (61.0 / 1.58 = 38.6) span one whole refill. Fewer would follow the
  luck of single takes; more would remember a bucket that has since
  refilled.
- *Stored* in 16 bits of 1/1024 ban, the finest step in which 63 bans
  fit, 1.5 ms.
- *Why the next hop's told waits are left out.* Built first with them
  in, the ring of eight relays at their cap, where each waits for the
  next, compounded them: each relay's service time held its next hop's
  line, which held the next one's, and the lines reached the 63-ban cap;
  the ring test's settled delivery fell from above 0.95 to 0.76 (seed
  1). Left out, the ring is round two's again: settled
  delivery 0.928, 0.943, 0.926 for seeds 1–3, round two 0.915, 0.902,
  0.935. The cost: a relay whose next
  hop refuses it often spaces its own senders by less than the time the
  way really takes, and the rest of the next hop's ban stands beside the
  line as a floor (G).

**G. The line per neighbour.** Each neighbour entry keeps when its line
ends. A refusal for want of the way on B, at the gate or of fresh work
(a sleepy node's own message at its anchor, or the node's own
application's) while B is banned, tells the rest of B's line plus one
service time, and B's line end moves out by that service time (spec
"Bans"). A line end in the past is now, so time shortens the line by
itself; neither the line nor a place passes 63 bans. The service time
used is F's, or how long the message that bars the way has waited for B
here, B's told waits aside, if that is longer, so that a rising load is
seen before the next take; and at least a ban.

- *Rounding.* A place is rounded up to whole bans, so that the sender
  never comes back before its place: early it costs a frame and a
  refusal, which is what broke round two. A wait passed on is still
  rounded down, so that it only shrinks around a ring.
- *At least a ban apart.* The ACK tells whole bans; places closer than
  that would be told the same wait and come back together.
- *The ring argument.* The rest of B's ban, a wait passed on, stands
  outside the line as a floor: a told wait is the later of the place and
  the ban's end. The line is this relay's own measure, which leaves out
  B's told waits (F), so it does not compound from relay to relay.
- *What it replaces.* The gate's told wait, the rest of the next hop's
  ban and at least the bucket's time for what is queued for it: the
  service time holds the bucket's waits. Fresh work's "rest of the ban
  on the way on" is now the floor. *Kept*: a refusal for want of room
  still takes a place in the bucket's line, a ban apart, and asks no
  less than the rest of its next hop's ban (round one's rule 3 as round
  two re-expressed it). The service time does not subsume it: a ban just
  told is the present, an average is not, and the room line is spaced by
  the bucket, not by a way.

**H. The one-way link.** A forward whose hand-over fails at a neighbour
that the failure leaves demoted, three hand-overs in a row unanswered
with no answer to this relay between, or one more at a neighbour already
demoted, counts that neighbour tried while it is queued here, and its
search goes on: to the next candidate, back to its parent, or, at the
origin, it ends (spec "Next hop"). The neighbour stays a last resort for
every message that has not tried it. The mark is kept with the queued
message (`Outgoing::passed`), not in the record, because the neighbour
does not have the message and a copy it offers later must be answered
seen, not taken as a return (roadmap D35, D90). The spec's paragraph
on the stall is gone from "Why the search finds a path", and the Bounds
row "a message held on one neighbour that does not answer, at most
14.2 s" now holds for the only candidate too.

- *What it costs.* Where three collisions demote a working link that is
  a relay's only way on, the message that met them is returned, or at
  its origin dropped; the next message uses the link as its last resort.
  The line tests that waited for a dead neighbour until its entry
  expired now see the message end at the demotion, 14 s instead of
  390 s.

**Memory.** The neighbour entry grows 80 → 104 B (service time, last
take, told waits, line end), the send-queue entry 280 → 296 B (where it
waits and since when, the neighbours passed), the relay 171 272 →
177 496 B, 61 KiB under the board's 234 KiB (`core-interface.md`
section 6).

**Counters.** `Trace::InLine`, for counting only: each place given, with
the wait told and the service time that spaced it, and whether the
relay was at its cap. The simulator bins them (`metrics::Pressure`
`line_waits`, `line_service`, `line_service_at_cap`).

## Round three: what it shows

**The line does what the user asked of it, and the storm is mostly
gone.** In the city at three times the gate refuses 153 thousand DATA
against round two's 3.0 million; the waits told spread over the bins
instead of piling at one ban (27 thousand places at one ban, 12
thousand at 32–63); offers per accepted text fall from 172 to 34; the
air of the run from 4.2 to 1.34 times main's. 95.3 % of the accepted
arrive, 97.6 % of them within 20 minutes, and the backlog grows by about
300 every ten minutes against main's 150 and round two's 2 500.

**S4 at three times is round three's best case.** One collision domain,
where the gate's refusals are mostly sleepy nodes' own messages at their
anchors: 81.7 % of the accepted arrive against main's 78.3 % and round
two's 42.3 %, 89.5 % within 20 minutes, the best of every build; the
origins refuse 17.8 % of texts (main 11.0, gateless 7.5), which is the
pushback the user asked for, and 5 868 texts arrive each ten minutes
against main's 6 121 and the gateless 6 859. Round two without the gate
still delivers more of the accepted, 82.8 %, with less air.

**At four times in the city it fails.** 63.0 % of the accepted arrive,
better than round two's 45.4 % and worse than main's 95.1 %, round
one's 84.8 % and the gateless 80.2 %; 7 577 texts each ten minutes
against main's 11 669. The gate still refuses 2.3 million DATA, and most
of the places are spaced by a service time under a ban, floored to one:
the senders return a ban apart, one is let in, and, most likely, the
next finds the place taken by a message that cut in with a higher count
or came unrefused from another sender, and is refused again; the runs
do not count which. The line spaces
only the senders this relay has refused; it does not hold back those it
has not.

**The gate is the cost, not the line or H.** Without H the city at three
times is worse, 94.7 % and the median text 47 s; with it 95.3 % and
35 s, so ending the wait at a demotion helps. The gap to round two
without the gate, 96.8 % and 15 s, is the gate's: its refusals, the
failed hand-overs that grow with them (973 thousand against 606
thousand), and the longer searches (34 offers per accepted text against
21).

**The service times are short.** In the city at three times, of 155
thousand places, 48 thousand were spaced by under a ban and 36 thousand
by one; at relays at their cap mostly 2–7 bans. The averaged time of one
hand-over is small next to the ban, so the ban's floor, not the
measurement, sets most places. Where the measurement is long the waits
are long: 12 thousand places told 32–63 bans.

**At the design rate round three is main's.** Delivery, latency and air
are main's in the city, at twice its rate, in S4 and with walkers; the
application is told to wait a few hundred times an hour, fewer than in
round two.

## Round three: verdict

The user decides. This note's reading, against the user's priority,
pushback to the origin and latency over throughput, throughput reported
plainly:

- **What round three fixes.** The storm of one-ban refusals: the city at
  three times goes from 67 % to 95 % of the accepted delivered, S4 at
  three times from 42 % to 82 %, at a third and a half of round two's
  air. And the
  one-way stall, which now ends at the demotion, at no measured cost.
- **What it does not fix.** It is still worse than leaving the gate out.
  In the city at three times the gateless round two delivers 96.8 % with
  the median text at 15 s, round three 95.3 % at 35 s; at four times
  80.2 % against 63.0 %. The pushback round three adds reaches the
  origins (6.1 % of texts refused at four times against 2.9 %), but it
  is paid for with refused frames that the line does not stop, and
  latency is worse, not better, which is the user's other priority.
- **What this note believes wrong.** One message per next hop, enforced
  by refusing a full DATA frame, costs air in proportion to the
  overload, and spacing the refused senders does not change that while
  unrefused arrivals and higher counts keep taking the place. The
  measurement that shows it is the city at four times: 2.3 million gate
  refusals and 63 % delivered, against 80 % with no gate. Of the three
  rounds, the parts that stand up to three times the rate are round
  two's view, pick, reserve and counters, and round three's H; the gate,
  with or without the line, does not.

**I. The told wait spans minutes (roadmap D109).** The ACK's six bits
told whole bans, at most 63, 99.6 s, so a wait of minutes was told as
99.6 s and the sender came back to be refused again, a full DATA frame
and an ACK at the refuser each time. The bits now hold a code: 1 to 8
are 1 to 8 bans, and each code above is an eighth longer than the one
below, rounded down and at least a ban longer. Codes 1 to 16 are one ban
apart, so a line's places a ban apart keep codes of their own; code 63
is 3384 bans, 89 min. Steps of a ninth would reach 44 min. A wait passed
on is rounded down to a code, a place in a line up to one, so a sender
comes back at most an eighth late (spec "Packet formats", "The ACK",
"Bans", `budget::told`).

- *A format change, and the version byte stays 1.* The bits are where
  they were, their meaning is new: a node of the old meaning reads code
  40, 229 bans, as 40 bans, and comes back six minutes early. Roadmap
  D77 promises that a node speaks every format from 1 to its newest;
  keeping that promise would mean speaking both meanings and answering
  each DATA in its own. No node is in the field, only bench boards, so
  format 1 is redefined instead, **without a version bump; the user
  chose this, 2026-11-04**, and from the first node planted a change of
  what a field means takes a new format (spec "Packet formats").
- *What the codes uncovered.* A refusal for room tells the time until
  the bucket holds everything queued; with the hop places full that can
  pass the bucket's depth, and the code fell back to the delivery TTL,
  10 min, which the old 99.6 s cap had hidden. It now tells the bucket's
  refill of all of it (`Budget::refilled_at`, its own commit). In every
  run here that case never came up: the district at 32 and 64 times
  repeats itself to the message with and without the fix.

**J. The gate weighs the wait, not a count.** A relay refuses a message
to send on, fresh work and forwards alike, when its wait here would pass
a hop's budget, `HOP_WAIT` = 30 s: its next hop's service time, F's
with G's floor for a rising load, for each queued message that goes
there now. A message whose count is higher than every one of theirs is
taken whatever the wait, so the hop places' progress argument stands
unchanged, and the ring test passes. `NEIGHBOUR_PLACES` goes (spec
"Send queue").

- *The told wait.* A refused sender gets a place in the way's line:
  when its own hand-over here would begin, a service time after the
  last place given, at least a ban, or after the queue if the queue
  ends later. It is told to come back when that place is `HOP_WAIT`
  away, the excess, rounded up to a code; the rest of the way's ban
  stays the floor (spec "Bans").
- *The gate weighs the queue, not the line.* A place in the line is a
  promise to a sender that will come back; counted against the gate, it
  would refuse that very sender on its return, its own place standing in
  its way. Found while designing it, before any run.
- *Why 30 s.* Measured, not assumed: the city at the design's rate
  (`dense-power.toml`, seed 1, settled hour) with no budget at all, a
  scratch build. The gate weighed 191 183 messages: 99.6 % would have
  waited under 0.5 s, 99.9 % under 5 s, 99.99 % under 10 s, one 20 to
  30 s, none longer. From the message log, the time per hop, from the
  first take to the holder over the count, was 0.30 s at the median,
  1.6 s at the 90th percentile, 4.4 s at the 99th. 30 s is the first
  round figure above the longest wait, six times the 99.9th percentile:
  it refuses only a hop slower than the design's rate ever is. Texts
  take a median 8 hops (the count, the search included) and 15 at the
  90th percentile, so the budget bounds the queueing at 4 to 8 minutes;
  a bound reported, not the input.

**K. Where held mail waits, simulator only.** Each row of the message
log now names the holder, and says at the end of the run whether the
holder is the destination's anchor, whether the destination is still
sleepy, when it last changed anchor and how often, and whether the
holder and its new anchor hear each other. Its finding has its own note,
`holder-2026-11-04.md`.

**Memory.** The service time and the told waits a neighbour entry keeps
go from 16 to 32 bits, since a told wait now passes 16 bits of 1/1024
ban; they fit in the entry's padding, and the relay stays 177 496 B,
61 KiB under the board's 234 KiB (`core-interface.md` section 6).

## Round six: detours on the base, ablation

### The rules

Round five left one question open: against main before D106 (`9417ef6`,
"old main" here), the base costs at capacity, and it was not known how
much of that is the base's rule that a message waits for its neighbour
and how much is keeping every text alive. Three research builds of the
merged code (`a38821e`), each chosen at build time by a cargo feature of
`bark-proto` (spec "Next hop"); the default build is unchanged:

- **A, `detour-silence`:** a forward whose hand-over goes unanswered
  goes round that neighbour while the neighbour's ban lasts and is
  routed afresh to the next candidate at once. A refusal still holds the
  message at the refuser.
- **B, both features:** as A, and a refused forward goes round the
  refuser for the wait it told.
- **C, `detour-refusal`:** a refused forward goes round the refuser for
  the wait it told; a failure holds the message for its neighbour as the
  default build does.

In each, a queued forward keeps one bit per neighbour-table slot, 8 B
more per send-queue entry; a set slot is no candidate while that
neighbour is banned, unless that leaves no candidate, or only a last
resort the neighbour was not, when the message waits for the ban as
before. Demotion, the gate, the hop-count cut and the rest are the
merged code's.

*The premise, corrected.* The question was put as "old main moved on
after one failed hand-over", which A was built to test. Old main did
not: on silence it held the message for its neighbour and moved on only
after three hand-overs in a row had failed (D102); on a refusal it set
the refuser aside for that hand-over and chose again at once among the
neighbours not banned. Old main's rule is C's, with the time to live. C
is therefore the ablation of old main's detour; A and B are kept for the
record of what moving on after silence does.

## Round seven: the own message's detour

### The rule

Under D110 a refused forward or return goes round the refuser for the
wait it told; a node's own application's message does not. Refused, it
waits on its hold ladder (spec "Bans"): one ban doubled per refusal in a row, never shorter
than the wait told, so when it is routed again the refuser's ban has
ended and it goes back to the refuser. One research build, the cargo
feature `own-detour` of `bark-proto` on main `9c8b35f`, tests that
rule; the default build is unchanged:

- **`own-detour`:** a relay's own message, refused, keeps the refuser's
  bit and is routed again at once among the other candidates, as a
  forward is. Where no other is left that is no worse a last resort it
  waits on the ladder as before.
- **The ladder** still counts every refusal in a row, gone round or
  not: each is a frame and a reply on the channels round the origin,
  whoever sent it, so the hold that comes once no other neighbour is
  left grows with all of them, and an origin with more neighbours
  offers no faster for it.
- **A sleepy node's own message** is unchanged: refused, it goes back
  to its application with the wait told, and its one candidate is its
  anchor (spec "A sleepy node's own message"). Only relay origins
  have more than one candidate.

### What changed, and why

- **The rule bites where the first hop is full.** A relay origin's own
  message is refused before its first hop takes it 4 344 times in the
  district at 32 times in main, 6 196 at 64 and 24 371 in four hours of
  the city at four times; none at the design's rate. In the feature
  build 97, 90 and 99 % of these refusals go round at once. In main a
  fifth to a third also go elsewhere next, after the hold: the refuser
  was banned again meanwhile by another message's refusal, so the
  message still had its bit set when it was routed.
- **Going round halves the refusals at the origin.** In main the held
  message goes back to the refuser when the ladder ends and is often
  refused again; going round, the next neighbour mostly takes it. The
  origin's refusals fall 42 % in the district at 32 times, 27 % at 64
  and 57 % in the city at four times, and the origin is clear of its
  message sooner: p90 from taken to first sent on 56 → 29 s, 136 →
  103 s and 16 → 8 s.
- **In the city at four times this is time and air.** The relay
  origins' median falls 45 → 36 s and p90 248 → 198 s; the sleepy
  origins', whose messages share the same relays, 45 → 39 s; air per
  delivered text falls 7 %, 5.04 → 4.67 s, and the median path a hop,
  13 → 12. The reading: taken by a neighbour beside the full one, the
  text no longer stands in the full relay's queue and line, nor holds
  its origin's next text behind it. Refusals sent overall are the
  same.
- **In the district at 32 times it is nothing measurable.** Every
  overall figure is within the seeds' range of main; relay origins
  write 4 % more and wait 6 s less at the median, sleepy origins deliver
  3 % fewer and wait 3 s more.
- **In the district at 64 times the relay origins take room from the
  sleepy ones.** Clear of its message sooner, a relay origin's
  application writes 14 % more (5 278 → 5 992 per 10 min) and its texts
  are delivered 10 % more often (4 420 → 4 849); the sleepy origins'
  delivered fall 1.4 % (4 481 → 4 420) and their median rises 14 s. The
  network delivers 4 % more texts in all, but at capacity a relay origin
  is now let into a neighbourhood a sleepy origin's anchor would refuse,
  and the accepted texts delivered fall 2.6 points, 58.3 → 55.7 %, with
  4 900 more refusals sent.

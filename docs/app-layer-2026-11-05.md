# The app layer: keys, payload, card and mailbox

**Date:** 2026-11-05, revised the same day after the user's decisions.
**Status:** proposal; no code, spec or roadmap changed. Spec references
are to `protocol.md` by section name. It builds on the vocabulary and
the mailbox the user has decided: a **node ID** is a radio's 6-byte
factory number; an **address** is a position and a node ID, 13 bytes,
where the network delivers; an **identity** is a person's public key,
which lives in the app and never changes; a **card** is an identity,
an address and a display name, exchanged as a QR code, and a card the
app keeps under a local name is a **contact**.

## Summary

An identity is one X25519 key pair made from a 128-bit seed, which the
person backs up as 12 words. All cryptography is libsodium's fixed
constructions, the same bytes in Rust and Dart: `crypto_box` seals a
message between two identities, the sealed box carries a first message
to someone who does not know the sender, and `crypto_secretbox` seals
what passes between a person and their mailbox. A sealed message names
no sender; the receiver tries its contacts' keys. Its overhead is 41
bytes, plus 5 of header and 13 when the sender's address must travel;
the app's payload is at most 210 bytes, so that a mailbox can forward
it, and holds up to 163 bytes of text, the spec's 100-byte payload a
40- to 53-byte one. Nothing is acknowledged by itself: a person may
press "acknowledge", which costs what a short text costs. The mailbox,
optional, is the person's home node, a fixed relay given a key derived
from the identity: it cannot read the mail it keeps, keeps the last
weeks of it in flash, and on a fetch sealed with that key sends it on to
the person's current address. Each fetched message crosses the city
twice, so a share f of mail fetched takes the design rate from 23 texts
a person a day to 23 / (1 + f).

## 1. Keys

**Library constructions.** Every operation is one call of libsodium
(its documentation, "Public-key authenticated encryption", "Sealed
boxes", "Secret-key authenticated encryption", "Key derivation"):

| use | construction | bytes over the plaintext |
|---|---|---|
| a message between two identities | `crypto_box_easy`: X25519, XSalsa20-Poly1305, random 24-byte nonce | 24 nonce + 16 tag = 40 |
| a first message | `crypto_box_seal`: an ephemeral X25519 key, nonce derived from it | 32 key + 16 tag = 48 |
| a person and their mailbox | `crypto_secretbox_easy`: XSalsa20-Poly1305 under a 32-byte key | 24 nonce + 16 tag = 40, or 16 when the nonce is not sent (section 5) |

The receiver precomputes each contact's shared key once
(`crypto_box_beforenm`). A 192-bit random nonce never repeats in
practice under any number of messages, so two phones of one identity,
which share no state, can both send.

*Why not the earlier draft's composition.* It built the same things
from X25519, HKDF and ChaCha20-Poly1305 with key-derivation labels, a
two-key introduction and a nonce function of its own. Nothing in it was
known to be weak, but every one of those choices was a place to get
something wrong, a label spelt differently in Rust and in Dart, a
missing check, a reused nonce, that only this project would ever test.
The fixed constructions have one call each and give the same bytes in
every implementation, and thousands of projects run them. They cost 12
bytes more a message, 6 % of a 100-byte text's airtime.

**Implementations.** The app: the Dart package `sodium` 4.1 through
`sodium_libs` 4.0, the Flutter plugin that ships libsodium itself
(pub.dev, 2026-10-27 and 2026-07-11); it has `box` with `precalculate`,
`seal` and `sealOpen`, `secretBox`, `kdf` and `genericHash`. The Rust
tests and the node: RustCrypto's `crypto_box` 0.9.1 (with its `seal`
feature) and `crypto_secretbox` 0.1.1, both `no_std`. Checked
2026-11-05 against libsodium 1.0.18 on the host: a box, a sealed box
and a secretbox made by the Rust crates open with libsodium's
`crypto_box_open_easy`, `crypto_box_seal_open` and
`crypto_secretbox_open_easy`, tag first as libsodium writes it, and
`crypto_kdf_derive_from_key` gives the bytes of the `blake2` crate's
keyed BLAKE2b with the same salt and personalisation. So `bark-link`'s
vectors can come from Rust and be checked in Dart, as the link's are.

**The backup is 12 words; every key comes from it.**

```
master   = crypto_generichash(seed, 32 bytes)                  BLAKE2b-256
identity = crypto_box_seed_keypair(crypto_kdf_derive_from_key(master, 1, "bark-key"))
mailbox  = crypto_kdf_derive_from_key(master, 2, "bark-mbx")   32 bytes
```

The seed is 128 random bits, the security of X25519 itself (RFC 7748
§1); the identity is public on every card, so the seed must withstand
an offline search of that size. The phrase is the seed and 4 bits of
checksum, the first 4 bits of its SHA-256 (FIPS 180-4), as 12 words of
11 bits from a fixed list of 2048: 132 bits. The list, in the language
people use, is fixed with the first app; changing it would lose every
backup.

**No signatures.** A box opens only for its two identities, so it
proves its sender to its receiver, and the mailbox key proves a fetch
to the node. A signature, 64 bytes, would add only that a third person
can check it, which nothing here needs: a card is trusted because the
person scanned it, or because the contact who passed it on is trusted.
If a later feature needs that, a directory or sending to an area (O7),
it adds a signing key then, under a new version (section 2). *Given
up:* whoever steals B's private key can also make messages to B that
open as from anyone; such a thief reads all of B's mail and speaks as B
anyway.

**No forward secrecy.** It would protect recorded messages from a key
stolen later, at the cost of a fresh key exchange per pair, a round
trip before the first protected message, 32 bytes on every message, and
state that every phone of an identity would have to share. The phone
whose key is stolen keeps the history anyway. Not worth it here.

## 2. The payload

The first byte, in the clear, is the format: the high four bits the
version, 1, the low four the form. A receiver drops a payload of a
version or form it does not know.

| form | to | after the format byte | overhead |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0, boxed | a contact | nonce (24), `crypto_box_easy` output | 41 |
| 1, introduction | someone who may not know the sender | `crypto_box_seal` output | 49 |
| 2, fetch | the person's own mailbox | nonce (24), `crypto_secretbox_easy` output | 41 |
| 3, from the mailbox | the person's current address | `crypto_secretbox_easy` output; nonce derived, not sent | 17 |

Forms 2 and 3 are the mailbox's (section 5). A payload the app writes
is at most 227 − 17 = **210 bytes**, so that its mailbox can send it on
in form 3 within the network's 227 (spec "Packet formats").

**A boxed message names no sender.** The receiver tries the key of
each contact until the tag checks: one XSalsa20 block and a Poly1305
over at most 169 bytes a try, in libsodium's native code, a few
milliseconds over a thousand contacts (to measure). What a payload
holds for text under each way of naming the sender (header and records
from section 3):

| sender named by | overhead, bytes | longest text, address omitted | longest text, address included | text in the spec's 100-byte payload |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| full identity in the clear | 73 | 131 | 118 | 21 / 8 |
| 2-byte hint in the clear | 43 | 161 | 148 | 51 / 38 |
| **nothing; the receiver tries its contacts** | **41** | **163** | **150** | **53 / 40** |
| first contact, form 1, with the sender's card | 49 + card | 104 to 72 | | |

Trying costs no bytes and lets relays link nothing: an identity, or a
hint, fixed per sender, would tie together every message a person
sends, and the payload carries the sender's address encrypted so that
relays do not learn who sent it (spec "Phone link").

**A first message** is form 1, holding the sender's card (section 4).
The sealed box hides who sent it but does not prove it, so the
receiver shows its sender as unverified until a form 0 message from
that identity arrives, which only its holder can make. The sender uses
form 1 until a message from the receiver, of either form, has opened:
it was sealed to the sender's identity, so the receiver holds the
sender's card and can open a box. An introduction that names an
identity the receiver has verified is dropped.

So one message each way makes both sides send boxes, and the next one
each way verifies both. If A scans B's card and writes first, A's
introduction gives B A's card, B's answer is a box and verifies B at A,
and A's next is a box and verifies A at B; the other way round is the
same. If both scan and both write before either reads, both send
introductions, both then send boxes, and those verify both.

*2026-11-06, on the bench:* the sender used form 1 until it had
verified the receiver. Two phones that both held the other's card then
sent each other introductions all day, and neither was ever verified,
as only a box verifies. *Given up:* anyone holding both cards can make
an introduction that seems to come from the receiver, after which the
sender boxes to someone who may not hold its card; such a forger can
already make texts that show as that person's, unverified.

**No padding.** A payload's length tells a listener roughly a text's
length. Padding to one length would cost a 100-byte text the airtime
of the longest payload, 146.6 ms a hop for 114.6, and hide little: the
destination, the hop count, and on the first hop the sending node are
in the clear (section 7).

**Text encoding.** UTF-8 spends 2 bytes on a Cyrillic letter. A text
that the single-byte windows-1251 page holds whole, Ukrainian and
Russian letters, Latin, and the usual quotes, dashes, apostrophe and
№, is sent in it, one byte a letter; any other in UTF-8. The text
record's type says which (section 3). For texts written in Ukrainian
this nearly doubles what a payload holds.

## 3. Inside a boxed message

The plaintext of forms 0 and 1 is a header and then records:

| field | bytes | |
|---|---|---|
| flags | 1 | bit 0: an address follows; bit 1: the sender's identity is the greater of the two, compared byte by byte; others zero |
| sent | 4 | the sender's clock, seconds since 2026-01-01 00:00 UTC; on each phone later than any it sent before: `max(now, last + 1)` |
| address | 0 or 13 | the address of the node the sender writes from |

| record | bytes | |
|---|---|---|
| text | 1 + rest | type 1 (UTF-8) or 2 (windows-1251); runs to the end, so it comes last |
| text part | 1 + 1 + rest | type 5 (UTF-8) or 6 (windows-1251), the part's number and the count of parts, less one each, in the high and low four bits; runs to the end (2026-11-06) |
| acknowledge | 1 + 4 | type 3, the `sent` of the text it answers |
| card | 1 + 1 + 38 to 83 | type 4, the card's length, then its bytes as in its QR code (section 4) |

A new record type takes a new version. *2026-11-06:* the text part
came in version 1, as a receiver drops a message with a record it does
not read, which is what a version it does not know would do, and no
whole text changes a byte.

**Bit 1 gives a box a direction.** One box key serves both directions
of a pair, so a message of A's to B, sent back to A by anyone, would
open at A as B's; the bit, checked against the two identities, drops
it. It costs no byte.

**`sent` numbers a sender's texts.** It orders a conversation, names a
text an acknowledgement answers, and stops a replay: a receiver drops a
text whose contact and `sent` it already has, keeping the pair even
when the text is deleted. It is the sender's clock, not a counter, so
that two phones of one identity number apart without talking to each
other; they collide only if both write to one contact in the same
second. *The network's message ID* cannot name a text: it hashes the
destination, which the mailbox changes (spec "Packet formats").

**The address** is that of the node the sender writes from: the one
their phone is connected to, or was last. It is included until the
contact has answered a message that carried it, and again whenever it
changes: 13 bytes, 5 % of a text's airtime, saved on most messages; a
message without it says it has not changed. *2026-11-06, the user:* it
was the address on the sender's card (section 4), the home node's when
they have one, so a phone never learned where a contact with a home
writes from, and every reply waited in a mailbox for a fetch. Now a
reply goes straight to where the person is, which saves the fetch, at
13 bytes on a message after a change.

**Where a text goes.** The receiving app keeps the two apart: the home
on the contact's card, if any (section 4), and the latest address a
message was written from, with the age of the newest message from the
contact, counted from its `sent`, or from its arrival when `sent` is
ahead of the phone's clock, so that mail fetched late does not pass for
fresh. While that age is under 60 s, the text goes where they write
from: in a minute a person on foot, at a brisk 1.5 m/s, moves about
90 m, within the ≈750 m a relay hears a handheld in the street
(spec "Physical layer"), while a driver has already left that reach, so
no longer wait makes the guess better. Past it, with a home,
the person chooses for each text between where the contact was and the
home's mailbox; without one, it goes where they were. With a home and
nothing written from, it goes to the home; with neither, the contact
cannot be written to until they write (roadmap D132).

**Acknowledge.** The network acknowledges only each hop (spec
"Addresses"), and the app acknowledges nothing by itself. The person
may press "acknowledge" on a received text; the app sends a message
with an acknowledge record and no text, and the other side shows a mark
on the text it names. It costs what a short text costs: 51 bytes,
66.0 ms a hop. Nothing is sent again by itself either; the person can
send a text again, and the receiver drops the copy by its `sent`.

**Long texts** (2026-11-06, the user, as phones split an SMS). A text
longer than a message holds goes as parts, each a message with its own
`sent`, its own delivery and its own acknowledgement. Two ways name the
text a part belongs to: each later part carries the first part's
`sent`, 4 bytes, or the parts take consecutive `sent`, so that a part's
`sent` less its number and one is the first's. The second costs
nothing and is as exact: `sent` is unique on each phone, so no other
message of the sender's can hold a number in the run, and a part read
alone, in any order, names its text and, by the count, the parts still
to come. Its only cost is that the last part's `sent` runs ahead of the
clock by the count, at most 15 s. The number and the count share one
byte, four bits each, so a part costs one byte over a whole text and a
text has at most 16 parts: 159 bytes each in a box without an address,
2.5 KB in all, more than two thirds of a person's 23 texts a day. A
whole text keeps types 1 and 2, so one-part texts are as before. Each
part takes the encoding that holds it, so the Cyrillic before an emoji
keeps one byte a letter.

The sender cuts a part between two characters as a person sees them,
after the last space in the part's last quarter if there is one. The
receiver shows the parts that have come, in order, with a line in place
of each missing one, and the whole text once all are in; the person's
one acknowledgement answers every part that has come, one record each
in as few messages as hold them, and a part that comes later waits for
the next.

## 4. The card

| field | bytes |
|---|---|
| version, 2 | 1 |
| flags: bit 0, a home address follows | 1 |
| identity | 32 |
| when the home was set or cleared, as `sent` | 4 |
| the home node's address | 0 or 13 |
| display name, UTF-8 | 0–32 |
| **total** | **38–70 without a home, 51–83 with one** |

The app records the home node when the person sets it (section 5) and
puts its address on the card. *2026-11-06, the user:* without a home
the card carries no address. Any other node's address is only where
the person was when the card was made, and where they write from
reaches the other side in every message's header (section 3); a person
without a home can be written to once they have written. The time stays
without a home, as clearing the home changes the card too, and the
later card must win.

**QR code.** The text `BARK:` and the card in base32 (RFC 4648 §6,
without padding): base32's letters and digits are all in the QR code's
alphanumeric set, at 5.5 bits a character, so the text costs a tenth
more than raw bytes and can be pasted or typed where a QR code cannot
go. 83 bytes are 133 characters and the prefix 5: version 6 at error
correction M, 41 × 41 modules, holding 154 (ISO/IEC 18004, the
capacity table); a name of 20 bytes or less fits version 5, 37 × 37.

**When the home changes**, moved, replaced, set for the first time or
cleared, the person sends their card to their contacts: a message with
a card record, which replaces the contact's card if its time is
later. 48 + 83 bytes, 106.9 ms a hop, one per contact, sent
when the person chooses. Someone who holds the card and has never
written does not get it. A message to the old home waits at whatever
node owns that position until its place is needed
(spec "Delivery queue"), and nothing in the network finds the new one
(spec "Addresses").

## 5. The mailbox

**Home.** A person may set a node as their home, from the app over the
phone link. It must be a fixed relay, on USB and not carried, so that
its address stays (spec "Nodes"); the app says so if it is not. The app
gives the node the mailbox key (section 1) and nothing else: the node
cannot open the person's mail, which is boxed to the identity. A home
phone is not needed; one that is there and connected is just a phone
reading the node's inbox.

**The ring.** The node keeps every payload delivered to its node ID
(`AppEvent::Message`, `core-interface.md` §4), but a valid fetch, in
flash, numbered from 1: a slot of 256 bytes, holding the number (3),
the length (1), the payload (up to 227) and a 16-bit check, 16 to a
page. When the ring is full the oldest page is erased. The phone link's
Inbox reads from it: the phone reads items after the last it confirmed,
and the ring keeps them for fetches either way. So a home node's inbox
survives a reboot and is never full (O22): the oldest is overwritten
instead.

**The fetch** is form 2 to the home address, sealed with the mailbox
key:

| field | bytes |
|---|---|
| sent | 4 |
| the requester's current address | 13 |
| since: the number up to which it has every item | 3 |
| most: the most items to send | 1 |

62 bytes in all. The node takes it if it opens and its `sent` is later
than the last fetch it took, which it keeps in flash; that stops a
replay. `since` = 0 asks for the newest `most` items, for a phone just
restored.

**The reply.** The node sends the items after `since`, oldest first, at
most `most`, to the fetch's address, and then each new item as it
arrives, until `since` + `most` is reached or the next fetch comes. Each
goes as form 3: the item sealed with the mailbox key under the nonce

```
round (4) ‖ number (3) ‖ 17 zero bytes
```

where round is the fetch's `sent`. The nonce is not sent: the requester
tries the numbers `since` + 1 to `since` + `most` until the tag checks,
and so learns the item's number, which nobody else can. Each round
gives every item a new nonce, since a later fetch has a later `sent`,
and the phones' random nonces never meet these. The requester then
opens the item as the message it is, form 0 or 1.

*Why sealed again and not sent on unchanged.* Unchanged, a second reply
to the same address after a loss would have the same message ID as the
first and be taken for it by every relay that keeps its record (spec
"Phone link", "Message records"), and the requester could not tell an
item's number.

**What was fetched.** `since` says it: the node keeps nothing per
requester but the latest fetch's address and round. An item lost on the
way, or held at a former anchor because the requester's address changed
before it arrived (spec "Delivery queue"; `docs/holder-2026-11-04.md`),
leaves a gap, and the next fetch asks again from below it; items after
the gap come twice and the requester drops them by `sent`.

**When to fetch.** When the requester's node's address changes and then
holds for the delivery TTL, 600 s, and when the person asks. Not on
every change: a walker's anchor changes every few hundred metres, and an
address that has held a delivery TTL is one the network keeps mail at
for that long (spec "Nodes"). `most` is 23, the requester's node's
inbox (spec "Phone link"), so a reply never overruns it.

*2026-11-06, the user's decision:* the app fetches only when the person
asks, never by itself, so that the mailbox's traffic, a fetch and its
mail crossing the city again, is only what a person wants.

*2026-11-06, the user's decisions after a bench test:* the fetch has no
`most`, the node answers it once with every item after `since` or one
reply that says nothing is new, and an item that arrives later waits
for the next fetch; a reply's nonce holds its place in the round and
its plaintext the item's number, 20 bytes over the item, so the app
writes at most 207. The spec's "Mailbox" says what is done now; this
section is the proposal as it was.

**An application on the node.** The mailbox uses only what the phone
link uses: `AppEvent::Message` in, `AppCommand::Send` out while
`accepts_send` holds, `AppEvent::GivenBack` to wait and send again. It
changes nothing in the protocol core, and the simulator runs it as one
more application over the same interface, from a `no_std` crate both
build. The node holds one message of its application's at a time (spec
"Receiving DATA"): the phone's goes first, the mailbox's when the phone
has none.

**The phone link** gains one characteristic, Home: written, 0 clears
the home, 1 and the 32-byte key sets it; read, whether it is set and the
items the ring holds. A new characteristic is a new link version
(spec "Phone link", on versions). Until O23 the key crosses an open link,
so anyone within Bluetooth range can read it as it is set or set another. *2026-11-06:* D135
closed O23; only the paired phone reads or writes Home.

**Flash.** The 1 MiB is laid out to the page
(`bark-image/src/layout.rs`), so the mailbox's pages come from a region
that has them, a page for its state (the key, the last fetch, the
round, the phone's last confirmed number) and the ring:

| from | pages | ring holds | at 23 a day |
|---|---|---|---|
| the SoftDevice's 148 KiB, if the board's bootloader starts the application without it, which no one has checked (`docs/mesh-update-2026-10-25.md`), and the loader stops write-protecting it | 1 + 36 | 35 × 16 = 560 items | 24 days |
| the update's staging, 22 pages of coded pieces, leaving 13 pages, 208 pieces, 3.25 blocks of 64 | 1 + 8 | 7 × 16 = 112 items | 4.9 days |

A page is erased once per 16 items, so at 23 a day each page of the
smaller ring is erased every 8 × 16 / 23 = 5.6 days, 10 000 erase
cycles in 150 years. A slot write stops the CPU for 59 words × 41 µs = 2.4 ms, an
erase 85 ms (nRF52840 Product Specification, NVMC), done through the
MPSL timeslots as the store's writes are, once BLE runs.

**Code.** The image slot is 360 448 bytes with about 9 KB free.
`crypto_secretbox` 0.1.1, sealing and opening, built for the board
(`thumbv7em-none-eabihf`, `opt-level = "s"`, fat LTO, as `bark-fw`)
takes 4.8 KB; the node needs no `crypto_box`. The ring, the fetch and
the Home characteristic add an estimated 2 to 3 KB: about 8 KB, nearly
all the room left. Something must go: first trouble-host's `log`
feature, which the bench does not need in a release image; if that is
not enough, the log lines, 11 KB, kept only in the console build.

**RAM:** a slot's buffer, 256 bytes, the state, about 64, and the
cipher's stack, a few hundred: under 1 KiB. A ring could also back
every node's inbox and free its 5.2 KiB of RAM; that is for later.

**Work per fetch:** one secretbox open of 21 bytes, one 32-byte state
record written; per item, a slot read from flash, one secretbox seal
of at most 210 bytes and one `Send`. At even 100 cycles a byte, well
above what XSalsa20 and Poly1305 take in software, 227 bytes are
23 000 cycles, 0.35 ms at 64 MHz.

**Air.** A fetch costs 71.1 ms a hop, about four fifths of the spec's
text. Each item crosses the city a second time, 17 bytes longer: a
100-byte text's 114.6 ms becomes 123.6 ms on its way to the requester.

## 6. Several phones, one identity

Every phone restored from the 12 words has the same identity and
mailbox key. Neither knows what the other has sent; the phone that
sends a text keeps it. Each phone fetches from its own `since`; the
node pushes to the latest fetch's address only. A phone may send its
contacts' cards to its own identity, a box from the identity to itself,
to the home address, so that the others get them in the ring; the home
phone, if there is one, reads them there too. A phone restored from the
words has no contacts and no history until it fetches.

## 7. What listeners and attackers learn

The network is open, and everything outside the payload is in the clear
(spec "What the protocol omits").

| learned by anyone in radio range | not learned |
|---|---|
| a message's destination address, so the recipient's home or anchor and the node ID it is for | the sender's identity and address, past the first hop |
| each hop's node IDs; on the first hop, a DATA with hop count 0 comes from the node that sends it, so a listener near the sender's node knows it sent | any identity: a boxed payload names none, a sealed box hides its sender's |
| each node's node ID and position, from its heartbeats | the text, its `sent`, what a record is |
| the payload's length and its form: a first message, a fetch and a mailbox reply are each visible as one | which item of the ring a reply carries |
| a mailbox's traffic: a fetch arrives at the home node, and replies leave it for the requester's address, each 17 bytes longer than an item that arrived; the timing ties the home to the requester's current address | |

**An attacker without the mailbox key** can send fetches the node
rejects, replay old ones, which their `sent` rejects, and fill the ring
with junk addressed to the home node, which pushes out the oldest mail
as any sender's messages would, at the rate the network lets anyone
send. It cannot fetch, redirect a reply, or read an item. A thief of
the node gets the mailbox key, and with it what the ring holds, still
boxed to the identity.

The spec's line, "so that the relays that carry a message do not learn
who sent it", holds from the second hop on; it should say so.

## 8. Air

Per hop, at SF5, with the hop's 17.0 ms ACK (spec "Physical layer"):

| message | payload, bytes | per hop, ms |
|---|---|---|
| spec's text, 100-byte payload | 100 | 90.3 |
| 100-byte text, address omitted | 147 | 114.6 |
| 100-byte text, address included | 160 | 121.0 |
| acknowledge | 51 | 66.0 |
| card to a contact | 131 | 106.9 |
| fetch | 62 | 71.1 |
| from the mailbox, a 100-byte text | 164 | 123.6 |
| longest the app writes | 210 | 146.6 |

The spec's design rate, 23 texts a person a day, counts a 100-byte
payload, which now holds a 40- to 53-byte text, and loses nothing to
acknowledgements, as none is sent by itself. A pressed acknowledgement
is a short text. A message fetched from a mailbox crosses the city
twice: with a share f of messages fetched, the rate is 23 / (1 + f), 23
with no mailbox in use and 11.5 if all mail is fetched, less the
fetches, four fifths of a text each.

## 9. Proposed decisions and spec changes

**Roadmap rows**, for the user:

| | |
|---|---|
| D123 | **An identity is one X25519 key pair made from a 128-bit seed that the person backs up as 12 words of a fixed 2048-word list; every key comes from the seed through libsodium's key derivation; all cryptography is libsodium's fixed constructions, `crypto_box` between identities, the sealed box for a first message, `crypto_secretbox` with the mailbox; no signatures, no forward secrecy** (O1; libsodium; `docs/app-layer-2026-11-05.md`). Resolves O1. One call per operation and the same bytes in every implementation, checked between RustCrypto's `crypto_box` and `crypto_secretbox` and libsodium; the app uses libsodium itself (`sodium_libs`). A box proves its sender to its receiver and the mailbox key proves a fetch, so a signature's 64 bytes buy nothing; forward secrecy needs a round trip, 32 bytes a message and state every phone of an identity would share. 128 bits is X25519's own security. |
| D124 | **A payload is a format byte and one of four forms: boxed to a contact (41 bytes over the plaintext), an introduction in a sealed box (49), a fetch to one's mailbox (41) and a mailbox's reply (17); the app writes at most 210 bytes; a boxed payload names no sender, and the receiver tries its contacts' keys; no padding** (spec "Phone link"). Trying costs milliseconds and saves 32 bytes, and names nothing a relay could link. 210 = 227 − 17, so that every payload can come back out of a mailbox. Padding would cost a text up to 28 % more airtime and hide little, as destination, hop count and the first hop's sender are in the clear. |
| D125 | **A boxed message's plaintext is a flags byte, `sent`, the sender's clock in seconds and later than any it sent before, the address to answer to when the contact may not have it, and records: text (UTF-8, or windows-1251 when it holds the text), acknowledge, card; a flag bit gives a box its direction** (spec "Phone link"). `sent` orders, names a text and stops replays, without two phones of one identity sharing a counter; the network's message ID cannot, as the mailbox changes the destination it hashes. Single-byte Cyrillic nearly doubles a Ukrainian text's room. |
| D126 | **The app acknowledges and resends nothing by itself: the person may press "acknowledge", which sends a record naming the text by `sent`, at the cost of a short text, and may send a text again** (spec "Addresses"). The design rate keeps its 23 texts a person a day; an automatic acknowledgement would cost two thirds of a text for each one. |
| D127 | **A card is version, identity, address, the address's time and a name of up to 32 bytes, 50 to 82 bytes, shown as `BARK:` and base32 in a QR code of version 6 or less at error correction M; its address is the home node's when the person has one, else the carried node's current one; when it changes the person sends their card to their contacts.** Base32 is in the QR code's alphanumeric set, so the card is also text that can be pasted, for a tenth more modules. A card in a message is the same bytes. |
| D128 | **A person may set a fixed relay as their home, giving it a mailbox key derived from the identity; the node keeps every payload for its node ID in a ring in flash, which its phone link's inbox reads, and on a fetch sealed with that key, newer than the last, sends each item after the fetch's number, sealed again with the key under a nonce of the fetch's time and the item's number, to the fetch's address; the requester fetches when its address has held for a delivery TTL and when the person asks (2026-11-06: only when the person asks); the mailbox is an application over the core's interface, not a change to the core** (spec "Phone link", "Capacity and sizing"; `core-interface.md` §4). Not everyone has a spare phone. The node cannot read the mail; sealed again, a second reply is a new message, and its number reaches only the requester. `since` keeps the node free of state per requester and bounds a loss by the next fetch. The ring's pages come from the SoftDevice's region, if the bootloader runs without it, else 8 of the update's staging. |

**Spec changes**, for the user:

- **"Phone link"**: "The payload" takes the forms of section 2, the
  header and records of section 3 and the card of section 4, as rules,
  with this note cited for reasons. Its "so that the relays that carry
  a message do not learn who sent it" becomes "so that the relays past
  the first hop do not learn who sent it: a DATA with hop count 0 names
  its sending node". The Home characteristic joins the service table,
  under link version 2, and Receiving says that a home node's inbox is
  its ring.
- **A new section "Mailbox"** after "Phone link": section 5's rules,
  the ring, the fetch, the reply and its nonce, when to fetch.
- **"Capacity and sizing"**, a paragraph after the design rate: "The
  app's encryption and header take 47 to 60 bytes of a payload, so the
  100-byte payload above holds a text of 40 to 53 bytes. A message
  fetched from a mailbox crosses the city twice: with a share f of
  messages fetched, the design rate is 23 / (1 + f) texts a person a
  day."
- **"Implementation notes"**: the mailbox's pages in the flash layout.
- **"Open items"**: O1 resolved by D123 to D128, moved to the roadmap's
  resolved list; O22 notes that a home node's inbox never fills; O23
  adds the mailbox key.
- **"What the protocol omits"**, "No authentication": add that a
  listener near a home node can tie it, by timing, to the address its
  mail goes on to.

## Unsure

- **Where the ring's pages come from.** The SoftDevice's region needs a
  bench check that the board's bootloader starts the application
  without it; the staging's needs the mesh update to live with 13
  pages.
- **The code room.** 4.8 KB is measured; the 2 to 3 KB of mailbox
  logic and what trouble-host's `log` feature frees are estimates.
- **The cost of trying contacts**, through Dart's binding to libsodium,
  for a thousand contacts.
- **The fetch's 600 s.** It is the delivery TTL, not a measured time a
  carried node keeps its anchor; the holder note saw still nodes change
  anchors two to four times in half an hour. Moot since 2026-11-06:
  the app fetches only when the person asks.
- **A phone's clock.** `sent` needs one that does not run far behind;
  the `max` rule keeps numbers rising after a jump back. The node has
  GPS time, which the link does not carry.
- **windows-1251** covers Ukrainian and Russian with the usual
  typography, not every text; others go as UTF-8.
- **The QR capacities** are from the standard's table as I recall it.
- **A home node's flood.** Anyone can push a person's oldest mail out
  of the ring by writing to the home node; the ring's size is the only
  defence.

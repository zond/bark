# The app layer: keys, payload, card and mailbox

**Date:** 2026-11-05. **Status:** proposal for the user's decision; no
code, spec or roadmap changed. Spec references are to `protocol.md` by
section name. It builds on the vocabulary and the mailbox the user has
decided: a **node ID** is a radio's 6-byte factory number; an
**address** is a position and a node ID, 13 bytes, where the network
delivers; an **identity** is a person's public key, which lives in the
app and never changes; a **card** is an identity, its home address and
a display name, exchanged as a QR code, and a card the app keeps under
a local name is a **contact**.

## Summary

An identity is one X25519 key pair (RFC 7748), made from a 128-bit seed
that the person backs up as 12 words. Every pair of identities shares a
fixed key in each direction, derived once from their static
Diffie-Hellman secret with HKDF-SHA256 (RFC 5869), and every message is
sealed with ChaCha20-Poly1305 (RFC 8439) under a fresh random 96-bit
nonce. No signatures: everything that must be authenticated is
authenticated by a key its two ends already share, which saves 64 bytes
a signature and a second key type. A payload names no sender; the
receiver tries the keys of its contacts, which costs microseconds and
no bytes. Only the first message to someone who does not know the
sender carries the sender's identity, encrypted to the receiver. The
overhead is 29 bytes, plus 5 of header and 13 when the sender's address
must travel, so a payload holds up to 192 bytes of text, and the
spec's 100-byte payload a 52- to 65-byte text. Inside the ciphertext
are records: a text, a delivered acknowledgement naming texts by their
sender's clock, a contact, and a fetch. The mailbox is a numbered
stream of everything sent to the identity: the home phone keeps it,
acknowledges each text as delivered, and re-seals each item to the
person's current address, with a nonce from which the person's phone,
and nobody else, reads the item's number; a fetch asks for the stream
after a number. A fetched message crosses the city twice, and a
delivered acknowledgement costs two thirds of a text, which together
take the design rate of 23 texts a person a day down to between 7 and
14, depending on how many go through a mailbox (below, "Air").

## 1. Keys

**One key pair, X25519, no signatures.** An identity is the 32-byte
X25519 public key A of a private key a (RFC 7748 §5). Two identities A
and B share `X25519(a, B) = X25519(b, A)`, and from it a key for each
direction:

```
K(A→B) = HKDF-SHA256(salt = none, IKM = X25519(a, B),
                     info = "bark pair 1" ‖ A ‖ B), 32 bytes
```

A message from A to B is sealed under K(A→B). Only A and B can compute
it, so a message that opens under it was made by one of them, and the
direction in `info` means it was made by A: a message of A's sent back
to A does not open as B's. An identity is its own contact, with
K(A→A), which both of its phones hold and nobody else does; the mailbox
(section 5) uses it. A peer key whose shared secret is all zeros is
refused when the card is read (RFC 7748 §6.1).

*Why no signatures.* The two places the user's sketch signs are a fetch
and an address update. A fetch goes from the identity to itself, so
K(A→A) authenticates it; an address update goes to each contact, so
that contact's pair key does. A signature, 64 bytes (RFC 8032 §5.1.6),
would add only that a third person can check it, which nothing here
needs: a card is trusted because the person scanned it, or because the
contact who passed it on is trusted. Signatures cost a second key type,
or one key used for both (an Ed25519 key converted to X25519 by the
map of RFC 7748 §4.1, one field inversion), and either way a
32-byte-larger card or first message, or a second scheme on one key.
If a later feature needs a statement anyone can check, a directory or
sending to an area (O7), it adds a signing key then, under a new
version (section 2).

*What it gives up.* Whoever steals B's private key can also make
messages to B that open as from anyone (key-compromise impersonation);
a thief who has B's key reads all of B's mail and speaks as B anyway,
so this adds little. And a receiver cannot prove to a third person who
wrote a message, which is as a private conversation should be.

**Cipher: ChaCha20-Poly1305** (RFC 8439 §2.8), 256-bit key, 96-bit
nonce, 128-bit tag. A pair key is used for every message in its
direction, so each message takes a random nonce. 96 random bits keep
two nonces of one key apart: at a million messages in one direction,
far beyond a pair's lifetime at 23 a day, two collide with probability
about n²/2⁹⁷ ≈ 6 × 10⁻¹⁸. The prototype's payload already begins with
such a nonce (`bark-app/lib/payload.dart`).

*Why random and not a counter.* Two phones of one identity send under
the same pair keys (section 6) and share no state, so a counter would
repeat across them, and a repeated nonce under ChaCha20-Poly1305 gives
away both plaintexts' XOR and the means to forge. A random nonce needs
no coordination. *AES-GCM* has the same sizes; ChaCha20 runs in
constant time in plain software, so a Dart app on any phone is safe.

**Keys do not change: no forward secrecy.** Forward secrecy would
protect recorded messages from a key stolen later. It needs a fresh
Diffie-Hellman exchange in each direction, a round trip before the
first protected message and 32 bytes in every message after, 14 % of
the payload, plus state per pair that both phones of an identity would
have to share and the home phone would need to re-seal mail (section
5). The phone whose key is stolen keeps the message history anyway.
Not worth it here.

**The backup is 12 words.** The private key, and every key above, comes
from a 128-bit random seed:

```
a = HKDF-SHA256(salt = none, IKM = seed, info = "bark identity 1"), 32 bytes
```

clamped as RFC 7748 §5 says. 128 bits is the security of X25519 itself
(RFC 7748 §1); the identity is public on every card, so the seed must
withstand an offline search of that size. The phrase is the seed and 4
bits of checksum, the first 4 bits of its SHA-256 (FIPS 180-4), as 12
words of 11 bits from a fixed list of 2048: 132 bits. The list, in the
language people use, is fixed with the first app; changing it would
lose every backup.

## 2. The payload

**Two forms.** The first byte, in the clear, is the format: the high
four bits the version, 1, the low four the form. A receiver drops a
payload of a version or form it does not know.

| form 0, sealed: to a contact | bytes |
|---|---|
| format | 1 |
| nonce, random | 12 |
| ciphertext of the plaintext (section 3) | n |
| tag; the format byte is associated data | 16 |
| **overhead** | **29** |

| form 1, introduction: to someone who may not know the sender | bytes |
|---|---|
| format | 1 |
| E, a fresh X25519 public key | 32 |
| the sender's identity A, encrypted (ChaCha20, RFC 8439 §2.4, key k₁, nonce 0) | 32 |
| ciphertext of the plaintext, ChaCha20-Poly1305 under k₂, nonce 0 | n |
| tag; format, E and the encrypted A are associated data | 16 |
| **overhead** | **81** |

with `es = X25519(e, B)`, `ss = X25519(a, B)`,
`k₁ = HKDF(es, "bark intro key 1" ‖ E ‖ B)` and
`k₂ = HKDF(es ‖ ss, "bark intro 1" ‖ E ‖ A ‖ B)`. The receiver finds A
with es, then checks the tag with ss: an A changed in the air gives
another ss and fails. Each key is used once, so nonce 0 is safe, and E
makes the payload unique.

A sender uses form 1 to a contact until it has received a form 0
message from it, which proves the contact knows its identity.

**No sender identity on a sealed message.** The receiver tries the pair
key of each contact, its own included, until the tag checks. A failed
try costs one ChaCha20 block, for the one-time Poly1305 key, and a
Poly1305 over at most 198 bytes: microseconds natively, a few
milliseconds over a thousand contacts in Dart (to measure). The table
compares what a payload holds for text under each way of naming the
sender (header and records from section 3):

| sender named by | overhead, bytes | longest text, address omitted | longest text, address included | text in the spec's 100-byte payload |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| full identity in the clear | 61 | 160 | 147 | 33 / 20 |
| 2-byte hint in the clear | 31 | 190 | 177 | 63 / 50 |
| **nothing; the receiver tries its contacts** | **29** | **192** | **179** | **65 / 52** |
| first contact, form 1 | 81 | | 127 | |

Trying costs no bytes and lets relays link nothing: a full identity, or
a hint, which is fixed per sender, would tie together every message a
person sends, and the payload carries the sender's address encrypted
precisely so that relays do not learn who sent it (spec "Phone link").

**No padding.** A payload's length tells a listener roughly a text's
length, and an acknowledgement from a text. Padding to one length
would cost a 100-byte text the airtime of a 227-byte one, 155.5 ms a
hop for 108.2 ms, and hide little: the destination, the hop count, and
on the first hop the sending node are in the clear (section 7).

**Text encoding.** UTF-8 spends 2 bytes on a Cyrillic letter. A text
that the single-byte windows-1251 page holds whole, Ukrainian and
Russian letters, Latin, and the usual quotes, dashes, apostrophe and
№, is sent in it, one byte a letter; any other in UTF-8. The text
record's type says which (section 3). For texts written in Ukrainian
this nearly doubles what a payload holds.

## 3. Inside the ciphertext

The plaintext is a header and then records:

| field | bytes | |
|---|---|---|
| flags | 1 | bit 0: an address follows; bit 1: it is the sender's home address; others zero |
| sent | 4 | the sender's clock, seconds since 2026-01-01 00:00 UTC; on each phone later than any it sent before: `max(now, last + 1)` |
| address | 0 or 13 | the address to answer to |

| record | bytes | |
|---|---|---|
| text | 1 + rest | type 1 (UTF-8) or 2 (windows-1251); runs to the end, so it comes last |
| delivered | 1 + 1 + 4k | type 3, k, then the `sent` of each of k texts received from this contact |
| contact | 1 + 32 + 13 + 4 + 1 + m | type 4: a card's identity, home address, address time and name (section 4); only to oneself |
| fetch | 1 + 3 + 1 | type 5: since, the stream number (section 5); most, the most items to send; only to oneself |
| name | 1 + 1 + m | type 6: the sender's display name, in a first message |

A message may hold several records, a delivered with a text the usual
pair. A new record type takes a new version.

**`sent` numbers a sender's texts.** It orders a conversation, names a
text in a delivered record, and stops a replay: a receiver drops a text
whose contact and `sent` it already has, keeping the pair even when the
text is deleted. It is the sender's clock and not a counter so that two
phones of one identity number apart without talking to each other;
they collide only if both write to one contact in the same second,
while the person is in two places. *The network's message ID* cannot
name a text: it hashes the destination, and the mailbox sends a text on
under another (spec "Packet formats"). A clock that jumps back cannot
reuse a number, by the `max`.

**The address** is the sender's home address when it has a home node
(section 5), else its node's current address. It is included until the
contact has acknowledged a message that carried it, and whenever it
changes: 13 bytes, 7 % of a text's airtime, saved on most messages.
With bit 1 set it also replaces the home address on the sender's
contact, if `sent` is later than that address's time; a message with
that and no record is an **address update**, which a phone sends to
every contact when its home address changes, the node moved or replaced.
It is 47 bytes, 63.4 ms a hop, once per contact.

**Delivered.** The network acknowledges only each hop (spec
"Addresses"), so the app acknowledges each text: the phone that first
stores it, the home phone in mailbox mode (section 5), sends a
delivered record to the text's sender, naming it by `sent`. A phone
keeps at most one acknowledgement waiting per contact: texts that
arrive while it waits join it, and a text to that contact takes it
along, so a conversation's acknowledgements ride on its replies and
only one-way traffic pays for them alone. No timer: the phone is the
queue (spec "Phone link"), and the acknowledgement goes when the node
takes it. Alone it is a 40-byte payload, 59.5 ms a hop with its hop's
ACK against a 100-byte text's 108.2 ms, two thirds of the spec's
90.3 ms text. A forwarded text is not acknowledged again, and nothing
acknowledges an acknowledgement.

A text with no delivered mark is shown as not yet delivered, and the
app does not send it again by itself: it would most often go to the
same stale address (section 4). The person can send it again; the
receiver drops the copy by its `sent`.

## 4. The card

| field | bytes |
|---|---|
| version | 1 |
| identity | 32 |
| home address, position (7) and node ID (6) | 13 |
| address time, as `sent` | 4 |
| display name, UTF-8 | 0–32 |
| **total** | **50–82** |

**QR code.** The text `BARK:` and the card in base32 (RFC 4648 §6,
without padding): base32's letters and digits are all in the QR code's
alphanumeric set, at 5.5 bits a character, so the text costs a tenth
more than raw bytes and can be pasted or typed where a QR code cannot
go. 82 bytes are 132 characters and the prefix 5: version 6 at error
correction M, 41 × 41 modules, holding 154 (ISO/IEC 18004, the
capacity table); a name of 20 bytes or less fits version 5, 37 × 37.

**A stale card.** A card's address goes stale when the home node moves
or is replaced; its contacts get an address update (section 3), but
someone holding the card who has never written does not. A message to
the old address waits at whatever node owns that position until its
place is needed (spec "Delivery queue"), and no delivered mark comes.
The app shows the card's address time, and a text not delivered says
the address may be old. Nothing in the network finds the new one
(spec "Addresses").

**The first message to an unknown identity** is form 1, with the
sender's home address (flags bits 0 and 1) and a name record. The
receiver shows it from an unknown sender with that name, and keeps the
sender as a contact if the person accepts; its delivered reply is form
0, after which both use form 0. Anyone with a person's card can write
to them; the app can only let the person ignore it.

## 5. The mailbox

The home phone stays connected to the home node, on USB at a fixed
address, the address on the person's card. It is the identity's
mailbox; the person on the move carries another node and a phone with
the same identity, the **requester**.

**The stream.** The home phone numbers, from 1, everything addressed to
the identity that it receives: texts, delivered records, first
messages, address updates, and contacts its own person adds. It keeps
every item (227 bytes or less, about 5 KiB a day at the design rate),
and acknowledges each text as delivered (section 3): delivered means in
the person's mailbox. It does not send on what the requester itself
sent it.

**The fetch.** The requester seals to its own identity, under K(A→A)
with a random nonce, a message to the home address with flags bit 0,
its current address, and a fetch record: `since`, the highest number
up to which it has every item, and `most`. The home phone takes a fetch
only if its `sent` is later than the last fetch it took, which stops a
replay; since a fetch opens only under K(A→A), nobody else can make
one.

**The reply.** The home phone sends the items after `since`, oldest
first, at most `most`, to the fetch's address, and from then on each
new item as it arrives, until `since` + `most` is reached or the next
fetch comes. Each item goes **re-sealed**: a message under the pair key
it came under, an item the home phone makes itself, a contact, under
K(A→A), each with the nonce

```
nonce = HMAC-SHA256(K_fwd, round ‖ number)[0..12]      (RFC 2104)
K_fwd = HKDF-SHA256(salt = none, IKM = X25519(a, A), info = "bark forward 1")
```

where round is the fetch's `sent`, 4 bytes, and number the item's, 3.
The requester recognises an item and its number by computing the
nonces of `since` + 1 to `since` + `most`; to anyone else the nonce is
random. A first message is sent on as a contact item, then the message
re-sealed under the new contact's pair key, so that the requester can
open it by trying its contacts.

*Why re-sealed and not sent on unchanged.* Unchanged, the payload is
the one that arrived at home, so a second reply to the same address
after a loss has the same message ID as the first and is taken for it
by every relay that keeps its record (spec "Phone link", "Message
records"); and a listener who heard both could tie the person's home
to their current address. Re-sealed with a fresh nonce each round, each
item is a new message and unlinkable by its bytes. The home phone holds
the identity's private key, so it has every pair key, and this costs
nothing but the sealing. *A numbered wrapper* around the unchanged payload would
not fit: a 227-byte payload has no room for another 29 bytes.

**What was fetched.** `since` says it: the home phone keeps nothing per
requester but the latest fetch's address and round. An item lost on the way, or held at a former anchor because
the requester's address changed before it arrived (spec "Delivery
queue"; `docs/holder-2026-11-04.md`), leaves a gap, and the next fetch
asks again from below the gap; items after the gap that did arrive come
twice and the requester drops them by `sent`. A loss is bounded by the
next fetch. `since` = 0 asks for the newest `most` items, for a phone
just restored.

**When to fetch.** When the requester's node's address changes and then
holds for the delivery TTL, 600 s, and when the person asks. Not on
every change: a walker's anchor changes every few hundred metres, and
mail sent to an address that has held a delivery TTL can be expected to
reach it, as the network keeps a dark node's mail that long (spec
"Nodes"). `most` is 23, the node's inbox (spec "Phone link"), so that a
reply never overruns it.

*Push and fetch both.* Without the push, mail would reach a person
only when they fetch; without fetching after a move, the push would go
to a former anchor. Each wasted push is one crossing.

**Air.** A fetch is 52 bytes, 65.9 ms a hop. Each item sent on crosses
the city a second time (section 8). A fetch after each of, say, five
stops a day costs about three texts' air; the second crossings of the
person's mail cost as much again as the mail.

## 6. Several phones, one identity

Both phones hold the seed, so the same private key and the same pair
keys. Neither knows what the other has sent: the phone that sends a
text keeps it. Contacts are kept on both: a contact the requester adds
goes to the home address as a contact record under K(A→A), and one the
home phone adds or learns goes to the requester as a stream item. A
delivered record for the requester's text reaches the home phone and
goes on to the requester as any item; the requester matches it by
`sent` among its own texts.

The home phone stores the whole stream, the contacts, the last fetch
taken, and the address and round it replies to. The requester stores
its `since`, its contacts and its conversations. Two requesters work,
each fetching from its own `since`; the push goes to the latest fetch's
address only.

A phone restored from the 12 words has the identity and nothing else:
no contacts until the home phone sends them, no history.

## 7. What relays and listeners learn

Tied to spec "What the protocol omits": the network is open, and
everything outside the payload is in the clear.

| learned by anyone in radio range | not learned |
|---|---|
| a message's destination address, so the recipient's home or anchor and the node ID it is for | the sender's identity and address, past the first hop |
| each hop's node IDs; on the first hop, a DATA with hop count 0 comes from the node that sends it, so a listener near the sender's node knows it sent | any identity: a sealed payload names none, a first message's is encrypted |
| each node's node ID and position, from its heartbeats | the text and its `sent` |
| the payload's length, so a text from an acknowledgement or a fetch, and the form: a first message is visible as one | which records a message holds, whether it is a fetch, an acknowledgement or a contact |
| timing: a home node that sends right after it receives ties the home to the requester's current address for a listener in range of the home node | which items of a mailbox are which; a re-sealed item cannot be matched to the one that arrived by its bytes |

The spec's line, "so that the relays that carry a message do not learn
who sent it", holds from the second hop on; it should say so.

## 8. Air

Per hop, at SF5, with the hop's 17.0 ms ACK (spec "Physical layer"):

| message | payload, bytes | per hop, ms |
|---|---|---|
| spec's text, 100-byte payload | 100 | 90.3 |
| 100-byte text, address omitted | 135 | 108.2 |
| 100-byte text, address included | 148 | 115.8 |
| delivered, one text, alone | 40 | 59.5 |
| fetch | 52 | 65.9 |
| address update | 47 | 63.4 |
| largest | 227 | 155.5 |

The spec's design rate, 23 texts a person a day, counts a 100-byte
payload and nothing else per text. A text and its acknowledgement
alone cost (90.3 + 59.5) / 90.3 = 1.66 texts; through a mailbox both
cross twice. With a share f of messages through a mailbox, the rate
becomes 23 / (1.66 (1 + f)): 13.9 texts a person a day with no
mailbox, 6.9 if everything goes through one, and up to 1.66 times more
where acknowledgements ride on replies.

## 9. Proposed decisions and spec changes

**Roadmap rows**, for the user:

| | |
|---|---|
| D123 | **An identity is one X25519 key pair, made by HKDF-SHA256 from a 128-bit seed that the person backs up as 12 words of a fixed 2048-word list; two identities share a fixed key in each direction, HKDF of their X25519 secret and both identities; messages are sealed with ChaCha20-Poly1305 under a random 96-bit nonce; no signatures and no forward secrecy** (O1; RFC 7748, 5869, 8439; `docs/app-layer-2026-11-05.md`). Resolves O1. Every message that must be authenticated is authenticated by a key its two ends share, the identity's own for a fetch, so a signature's 64 bytes and second key type buy nothing; forward secrecy needs a round trip, 32 bytes a message and state both phones of an identity would share. Random nonces need no coordination between those phones. 128 bits is X25519's own security. |
| D124 | **A payload is a format byte, then sealed (nonce, ciphertext, tag: 29 bytes) or, to someone who may not know the sender, an introduction (an ephemeral key and the sender's identity encrypted to the receiver: 81 bytes); a sealed payload names no sender, and the receiver tries its contacts' keys; no padding** (spec "Phone link"). Trying costs microseconds and saves 32 bytes, and names nothing a relay could link; padding would cost a text up to half again its airtime and hide little, as destination, hop count and the first hop's sender are in the clear. |
| D125 | **The plaintext is a flags byte, `sent`, the sender's clock in seconds and later than any it sent before, the address to answer to when the contact may not have it, and records: text (UTF-8, or windows-1251 when it holds the text), delivered, contact, fetch, name** (spec "Phone link"). `sent` orders, names a text for its acknowledgement and stops replays, without two phones of one identity sharing a counter; the network's message ID cannot, as the mailbox changes the destination it hashes. | D126 | **The phone that first stores a text acknowledges it as delivered, naming it by `sent`; one acknowledgement waits per contact and rides on the next text to it; the app never resends by itself; a home address change goes to every contact as an address update** (spec "Addresses"). The network acknowledges only hops. An acknowledgement alone costs two thirds of a text, so it rides where it can; a resend would mostly go to the same stale address. |
| D127 | **A card is version, identity, home address, the address's time and a name of up to 32 bytes, 50 to 82 bytes, shown as `BARK:` and base32 in a QR code of version 6 or less at error correction M.** Base32 is in the QR code's alphanumeric set, so the card is also text that can be pasted, for a tenth more modules. |
| D128 | **The home phone is the identity's mailbox: it numbers everything sent to the identity, acknowledges each text as delivered, and sends each item, re-sealed with a nonce derived from the number and the fetch, to the address of the latest fetch; a fetch, sealed to the identity itself, gives the requester's address, the number up to which it has everything, and at most 23 to send; the requester fetches when its address has held for a delivery TTL and when the person asks** (spec "Phone link", "Capacity and sizing"). Re-sealed, a second reply is a new message, not taken for the first by relays that remember it, and unlinkable to the original by its bytes. `since` keeps the home phone free of state per requester and bounds a loss by the next fetch. 23 is the node's inbox. |

**Spec changes**, for the user:

- **"Phone link", "The payload"**: replace the paragraph on encryption
  with the forms of section 2, the header and records of section 3 and
  the card of section 4, as rules, with this note cited for reasons;
  the mailbox of section 5 as its own subsection after it. Change "so
  that the relays that carry a message do not learn who sent it" to
  "so that the relays past the first hop do not learn who sent it: a
  DATA with hop count 0 names its sending node". The uniqueness
  paragraph stays; the random nonce, the introduction's ephemeral key
  and the re-sealed item's derived nonce each give it.
- **"Capacity and sizing"**, a paragraph after the design rate: "The
  app's encryption and header take 34 to 47 bytes of a payload, so the
  100-byte payload above holds a text of 52 to 65 bytes. A delivered
  acknowledgement sent alone costs 59.5 ms a hop, two thirds of a
  text's 90.3 ms, and a message fetched from a mailbox crosses the city
  twice. With a share f of messages through a mailbox, and
  acknowledgements alone, the design rate is 23 / (1.66 (1 + f)) texts a
  person a day: 13.9 with no mailbox, 6.9 with every message through
  one; acknowledgements that ride on replies give back up to the 1.66."
- **"Open items"**: O1 resolved by D123 to D128, moved to the roadmap's
  resolved list.
- **"What the protocol omits"**, "No authentication": add that the
  app's sealing makes no frame authentic, and that a listener near a
  home node can tie it, by timing, to the address its mail goes on to.

## Unsure

- **The cost of trying contacts** in Dart, for a thousand contacts; the
  estimate is a few milliseconds a message.
- **The air of acknowledgements and the mailbox.** Taking the design
  rate from 23 to between 7 and 14 is the largest effect here. If it
  binds, the levers are acknowledgements that wait longer for a reply to
  ride on, or no acknowledgement of a mailbox item's second crossing,
  which the design already omits; both trade a person's view of
  delivery for air.
- **The fetch's 600 s.** It is the delivery TTL, not a measured settle
  time of a carried node's anchor; the holder note saw still nodes
  change anchors two to four times in half an hour, so a requester may
  fetch more often than its moves suggest.
- **A phone's clock.** `sent` needs a clock that does not run far
  behind; a phone without network or GPS time keeps its own, and the
  `max` rule keeps numbers rising after a jump back. The node has GPS
  time, which the link does not carry.
- **windows-1251** covers Ukrainian and Russian with the usual
  typography, not every text a person writes; others go as UTF-8.
- **The QR capacities** are from the standard's table as I recall it;
  the app's QR library will tell.

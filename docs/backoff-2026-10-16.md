# Silence as pushback: measured

**Date:** 2026-10-16. Builds on `collapse-research-2026-10-16.md`, which
found that at three times the spec "Capacity and sizing" texting rate the hand-over breaks
first, and nothing slows a sender whose hand-overs go unanswered. This
note measures the rule of roadmap D42 in the two forms that were built,
against main `b86c3f5`, and the form kept against main `4ed5c0b` after
the leaf keepalive was rebuilt (D41). Spec references are to
`protocol.md`.

## What was built

Both forms share one change (commit `b750744`): a hand-over whose three
attempts go unanswered bans the neighbour for every message, as a
refusal does, for one ban doubled for each earlier failure in a row:
1.56, 3.11, 6.2 s and so on up to the bucket's refill of 61 s, the
ladder spec "Hand-over" already uses for a relay's own refused message. An ACK
resets the failure count and so the ladder. Messages whose every
candidate is banned wait, most travelled first when the bans end, and a
relay takes no fresh work while every live neighbour is banned (D34).
Refusals are unchanged.

They differ in what the failed message itself does (commit `ab91bb9`,
a switch for the measurement):

- **Reroute (R):** it goes to its next unbanned candidate at once, as
  today.
- **Wait (W):** it waits for that neighbour's ban and is offered to it
  again with three fresh attempts. It goes to the next candidate only
  once the neighbour is demoted, after the second failure in a row
  (spec "Neighbour table"). A return waits for the parent the same way and is dropped
  only when that failure demotes the parent.

W is the form kept (commit `32e759c`); R and the switch were taken out
once measured (commit `239027e`). For reference, **H** is the
throw-away experiment of the collapse note, run again on the same
seeds: after the k-th failed hand-over in a row the relay starts no
hand-over at all for the same ladder, and takes no fresh work meanwhile.
H is not proposed; it shows how much of the gain a per-neighbour rule
keeps.

## What was run

S4 (`scenarios/s4-capacity.toml`) at the texting rate times 1, 2, 3 and
4, seeds 1 and 2, with 13 channels of 125 kHz and with 6 channels of
250 kHz (noise floor 3 dB up), as in the collapse note. "Delivered" is
the share of reachable texts sent from 900 to 3900 s that arrived. The
standard suite: S1, mesh-burst, mesh-spread and the ring at 10 seeds,
S1b, S2 and the corridor at 60. The loaded scenarios of D31 to D36: the
corridor at one text per leaf every 1 s and 0.5 s, the ring at 1 s and
2 s, mesh-burst at 64 times, 30 seeds each. Differences are paired by
seed, ± one standard error. With two seeds the S4 errors say little;
the S4 runs are deterministic and repeat to the frame.

A message's **life** is from its origin's core taking it to its
delivery or drop. A leaf that cannot hand a text over holds it for the
ten-minute TTL and drops it (`NoRelay`), so over half the S4 texts at
×3 and ×4 have a life of exactly 600 s spent at the leaf; "in the
network" leaves those out.

With W the 250 kHz domain carries the ×3 load for 40 minutes and then
falls as main does, 30 minutes later; it does not settle at the most it
carried. At ×4 the cliff moves by one bin. At 125 kHz the fall is
slower but there is no plateau: the centre relays are at their duty cap
(52 % of relay samples at ×3), and silence is not what limits them.

H, which stops the whole relay rather than one neighbour, does as well
as W at ×3 and better at ×4 with 250 kHz (27.7 % against 11.3 %), with
fewer frames (11.5 DATA frames per text at 250 ×3 against 17.9) and
seed 2 at ×3 holding to the end. Stopping a relay that fails stops
its traffic to every neighbour, which W does only neighbour by
neighbour; whether that is worth stopping one good link for a bad
one is a question for a later decision, not measured here at light
load.

At the spec "Capacity and sizing" rate the tail is texts waiting in their relay's delivery
queue for a leaf that does not answer, about 700 of them a run dropped
there after 600 s (`QueueExpired`, spec "Delivery queue"). Frames per message that ended undelivered, mean: 22 at ×1 for
both, 60 → 53 at 125 kHz ×3, 105 → 94 at 250 kHz ×3, 69 → 90 at 250 kHz
×4.

**The backoff does not bound lifetimes.** Where W holds the load (250
kHz ×3, until the cliff) the longest life in the network falls from
55 to 20 minutes. Past the cliff, and at 125 kHz ×3 and both ×4 runs,
messages still live 48 to 58 minutes, as with main, and the oldest
still in flight at the end of the run is 53 to 64 minutes old. Nothing
in the backoff ends a message: a ban makes it wait, and a message that
waits is never dropped (spec "Hand-over", D31). In the collapse it is these
messages that keep the buckets dry. So a message still needs an end by
time in the network, the TTL byte of the draft decision in the collapse
note; the backoff and the TTL do different things.

## Refusals while a hop place was free

The question: should the refusal ACK carry the lowest count its relay
would take, so that the refused sender could offer another of its
messages instead? Counted at every refusal of a relay's DATA for the
bucket or a full room: did the refusing relay then have a free hop
place at a count the refused sender would send another message it held
to that relay with (a forward in its queue that has not been handed
there and whose parent is not that relay)? Waiting is from each refusal
to that message's next DATA from the sender.

| ring at 1 s, main / W (30 seeds) | 31 115 / 31 582 | 23.4 % / 21.7 % | 35 916 / 36 806 s | 30.7 % / 28.0 % |

Below the cliff such refusals are rare: at ×2 a sixth of a few thousand
refusals. Past it most refusals are of this kind, because a relay at
the cap refuses into its regular room while its hop places above the
counts it is offered stay free, and its neighbours hold messages that
have travelled farther. Naming the lowest count would let those move,
but they are the messages the TTL above would end; it does not touch
the loop that starts the collapse. It matters in the ring, a fifth to a
third, and not at all in the corridor, where a sender holds only
messages one hop behind the refused one.

Where the old rule dropped a message for want of a route, its three
attempts had collided at the receiver (S1b, the corridors, the rings);
now it waits for the ban and goes again, and nothing is dropped for
want of a route in any of these runs. The latency the corridors gain at
p90, 2 to 5 s, is that wait.

The ring at 2 s is the one row worse. Its two worst seeds with W, 14
and 19 (0.882 and 0.93), and with R, 4 and 15, are each a relay that
dropped a leaf from its leaf table after a lost poll answer and then
held every later text for that leaf until the run ended: 144 to 178
texts each. Main shows the same failure in the ring at 1 s, seeds 20
and 29. It belongs to the leaf keepalive of D39, since rebuilt as D41,
and which seeds it hits follows the timing of the run. The section
after next measures the ring again with D41 in.

**A neighbour that is gone.** S1 removes B, A's only way on. A's message
now waits for B's bans until A's entry for B expires, 390 s after B's
last heartbeat; meanwhile A takes nothing from its leaf, the leaf holds
its text, and its application skips the texts that fall due (in seed 1,
10 of the 20 after the removal). Every text that reached A is still dropped
there for want of a route; none could have been delivered. A relay
pays at most 11 failed hand-overs to a neighbour that is gone, whatever
the number of messages, against three for every message routed past
it before (spec "Bounds").

**A link that works one way only** is the case the rule does not end:
a neighbour whose heartbeats this relay hears but which never hears
it. Its entry does not expire, so a message whose only way on it is
waits, offered to it once a minute, for as long as its heartbeats are
heard. Before, the message went back to its parent after three
attempts. The suites above have no such link; the city model's links
are the same both ways apart from fading.

## Lost without a trace

Main lost none in these runs. W lost one message in S4 125 kHz ×4 seed
1 and one in 250 kHz ×4 seed 2; R one in 125 kHz ×3 seed 1 and two in
250 kHz ×4 seed 2. Each was run again with a print where the
simulator maps a message's ID to its traffic tag; the reruns repeat
the runs to the frame, and every unaccounted message is one of two
traffic tags that got the same 32-bit message ID (in the four runs, one
to three such pairs each). That is the simulator's known collision: its
table keeps one tag per ID, and nothing was lost in the protocol.

## Recommendation

Keep W, the default. It is the only form that keeps the effect of the
throw-away experiment, it leaves the spec "Capacity and sizing" rate and twice it unchanged
for under 3 % more airtime, and it ends every drop for want of a route
in the standard and loaded suites, before and after the keepalive
rebuild. R was taken out once measured.

Three things it does not do, measured here:

1. It does not bound a message's life. Past the cliff messages live
   for most of an hour, as with main. The TTL of the draft decision is
   still needed.
2. It does not hold the carried maximum: at 250 kHz ×3 the domain still
   collapses, 30 minutes later. Stopping the whole relay (H) did better
   at ×4, which suggests the rest of the gain is in pushing back on the
   relay's own traffic rather than one link's.
3. A one-way link holds the messages that have no other way on for as
   long as it is heard.

The refusal need not carry the lowest count its relay takes: it would
matter only past the cliff, for messages the TTL should end.

# Slotted access: what time-sharing could and could not cut

**Date:** 2026-11-02. **Status:** studied and measured in the simulator;
not built. Roadmap D101. Spec references are to `protocol.md`. Measured
on main `8ff3a74` with a simulator that also counts, for every frame
addressed to a relay and lost, what the receiver was facing when it lost
it (`bark-sim/src/metrics.rs` `Addressed::lost_how`,
`Addressed::heartbeat_late`). The counts change no behaviour: the
instrumented build reproduces main to the byte on the ring and on
`dense-power.toml` seed 1.

## Summary

- **The question.** Collisions in the dense city lose DATA frames and
  ACKs under random access with listen-before-talk. Could slots, or any
  other sharing of time, cut them, with no network time and no node
  knowing its neighbours' neighbours?
- **What the losses are.** In the dense city (`dense-power.toml` seed 1,
  the settled hour) 43.7 % of the DATA from relays to relays arrive.
  Of what is sent, 26.6 % is lost to noise alone, a link too weak at
  that moment with no frame within 7.5 dB of the noise at the
  receiver; 15.2 % to a receiver that was
  deaf, on another channel or demodulating a frame it had already lost;
  7.6 % to a DATA addressed to another node on the same channel; 3.3 %
  to heartbeats and beats; 1.9 % to ACKs; and **1.8 % to a DATA
  addressed to the same receiver**, the one class that
  receiver-anchored slots can reach. Under load (S4 at three times the
  design rate) that class is 8.3 %, against 10.9 % for DATA to another
  node and 12.0 % deaf or elsewhere.
- **Receiver-anchored slots would reach a fifth to a third of that
  class, not half.** The slot must hold the frame, the check, the
  transmitter's start and a guard for the clocks, so it is longer than
  the frame, and a slotted frame's vulnerable window is a slot where an
  unslotted one's is two frames: 0.79 of it for the 129-byte text, 0.66
  for a full frame, with the phase a rotation old. And slots take from
  the senders that hear each other the serialisation listen-before-talk
  gives them today for nothing; giving it back needs a contention
  window at the start of every slot, which makes the slot as long as
  two frames and leaves nothing. The gain is bounded at 0.4 to 0.6
  points of DATA reception in the dense city and 2 to 3 under load, for
  a byte more in every heartbeat, a latency of half a slot or more per
  hop, and a sender that must wait rather than send.
- **The busy estimate predicts nothing at the frame scale.** It is a
  30-minute mean of a reading every 13 s. The channel's occupancy has
  memory at the second scale, from retries, and the only occupants
  known in advance are the relays' heartbeats, 3.3 % of the dense
  city's DATA losses.
- **A second spreading factor is out:** the SX1262 demodulates one at
  a time. **Network time is out:** GPS time exists only on relays on
  external power with a sky view, and a grid copied from neighbour to
  neighbour is a time-sync protocol with a reference rule, which the
  design excludes; what it would be worth is bounded here all the same,
  2 to 4 points in the dense city, 5 to 8 under load, before the same
  contention cost.
- **Recommendation: build none of them.** The dense city's losses are
  links without margin and receivers that are deaf or elsewhere, not
  senders colliding at one receiver; the access rule is not where the
  frames go missing. The next study is the 26.6 % lost to noise.

## The question

The storm note (`storm-2026-10-30.md`), the ACK note
(`storm-ack-2026-10-31.md`) and the fates of `storm-wait-2026-10-31.md`
count, in the dense city, 42 % of the DATA addressed to relays lost
while the receiver was listening and free, 9 % while it received
another frame, and 14 % of the ACKs lost while the sender listened
free. Random access with listen-before-talk cannot stop two senders
that do not hear each other from meeting at one receiver. The user asks
whether a slotted or time-shared access could cut these, even though
the spec has no fixed plan and no time sync (spec "Summary"), perhaps
probabilistically, and what can be derived of it.

## The constraints

What the design gives a slot scheme to build on, and what it denies it:

- **No network time** (spec "Summary", "Position"). Each node's clock is
  its own. A relay on external power runs its GPS (roadmap D82) and so
  has GPS time when it has a fix; indoors, where most of the people's
  relays stand, the L76K mostly finds nothing (spec "Beats"); a sleepy
  node has only its anchor's heartbeats.
- **Clocks.** The nRF52840 times the radio from its 32.768 kHz
  low-frequency clock: the on-chip RC oscillator is ±500 ppm, and
  anything better needs the crystal (nRF52840 Product Specification,
  CLOCK, "LFCLK clock sources"), whose accuracy is the crystal's, about
  ±50 ppm for a watch crystal (spec "Heartbeat",
  `relay/anchor.rs` `CLOCK_DRIFT_PPM`); a cold board drifts more, since
  a tuning-fork crystal's frequency falls with the square of the
  distance from its 25 °C turnover. Two clocks part by up to 100 ppm:
  1.5 ms over a 15 s heartbeat interval, 19.5 ms over a worst-case
  rotation of 195 s, 39 ms over two.
- **Heartbeats.** A relay heartbeats every T = 5–15 s, fixed by its ID,
  a channel a slot (spec "Heartbeat"), so a neighbour that has heard
  one knows when every later one is due. But the heartbeat *starts* 0
  to 872.8 ms after its slot, by its listen-before-talk and whatever
  the radio was doing: in the calm dense city 95.2 % start within 40 ms
  of the relay's grid and 2.9 % more than 80 ms after it; in the storm
  at twice the rate 80.0 % within 40 ms; under load (S4 at three times
  the rate) 69.3 % within 40 ms and 17.7 % more than 120 ms after,
  measured below. A heard heartbeat is a loose anchor
  for a grid of frame-sized slots.
- **Channels.** A sender transmits on the receiver's home channel, where
  the receiver listens; the ACK comes back at once on the sender's home
  channel (spec "Home channel selection", "The ACK"). Several relays
  share a channel in one hearing range: in the dense city, 5 317 relays
  on 28 km², about 340 stand within 750 m of a street-level receiver,
  some 26 of them on its channel.
- **Frames** (spec "Physical layer", `radio.rs` `time_on_air`): a
  heartbeat 14.4 ms, an ACK 17.0 ms, the 129-byte DATA of a 100-byte
  text 74.6 ms, a full frame 138.6 ms, 372.8 ms with the long preamble.
  A listen-before-talk check takes 3.1 ms and a transmission starts
  1.42 ms after it is asked for (spec "Heartbeat"). The hand-over's
  backoff is 0–50 ms, its retry wait 0–311 then 0–622 ms (spec
  "Hand-over", "Retry wait").
- **The duty cycle** caps each node at 10 % of airtime
  (spec "Duty cycle cap"), so under load the bucket binds before the
  channel does (roadmap D93, D97).
- **The receiver** (`bark-sim/src/radio.rs`, `phy.rs`) locks on the
  first preamble it hears; a stronger one by 5 dB steals the lock
  during the preamble; every symbol may then be lost at a rate set by
  the signal against noise and interference, interference weighing 5.6
  times its power so that the 5 dB capture threshold and the
  sensitivity agree. A frame lost past its header leaves the receiver
  deaf until the frame's end.

## What the losses are

The simulator already counted the fate of every frame addressed to a
relay by what the receiver was doing when the frame started (received;
lost while listening and free; while receiving another frame; while
deaf; on another channel). This study adds what the receiver faced when
it lost the frame: noise, if noise outweighed the interference it saw;
else the strongest frame on the air at it, classed as a DATA addressed
to the same receiver, a DATA to another node, an ACK, a relay's
heartbeat or a sleepy node's beat; and a DATA to the same receiver is
split by whether the later of the two senders could hear the earlier
one's frame by the link table (an audible pair) or not (a hidden pair;
links with a walker are not in the table and count as hidden). A loss
that fits none of these is "other": most of it the receiver deaf after
a frame it had lost past its header, which the fates count as "free";
the rest adjacent-channel leakage.

(`storm3/dp-x2.toml`, which storms on this base: 88.8 % of the settled
hour's texts delivered at 1 118 451 s of airtime), and S4 at three
times the design rate (`per_leaf_per_hour` 2.875) seed 1, all settled
hours; `ring.toml` seed 1. Shares are of the frames sent.

the output (`how.py`); the noise losses are all "free", and most of the
"receiving" losses are DATA to another node.

What it says. In the dense city the frames go missing on the link, not
in the contest for it: a quarter of all DATA sent arrives too weak to
demodulate with nothing else on the air, and a further sixth finds the
receiver transmitting, checking, on another channel or still deaf
from a frame it lost. The collisions proper are 14.6 % of the DATA, and
most of them are with frames to *other* receivers on the same channel,
or with heartbeats and ACKs, none of which a receiver's slots order.
The class slots can reach, two DATA to the same receiver, is 1.8 %,
and nearly all of it hidden pairs, 1.7 %: listen-before-talk already
keeps the audible pairs apart. Under load it is 8.3 %, a fifth of the
losses, 7.8 % hidden and 0.5 % audible, with DATA to another node a
quarter and the deaf receiver another quarter. In the storm, the
dense city at twice the rate, the same-receiver class is 3.7 % while
collisions with DATA to other nodes are 22.4 %: the storm's copies
crowd every channel, and six collisions in seven are with frames a
receiver's own slots do not order. On the ring, a
line of relays each with a neighbour on either side that cannot hear
each other, same-receiver collisions are 5.2 % and every one a hidden
pair, as the geometry says; the receiver's own transmissions cost three
times as much.

## Candidate 1: receiver-anchored slots

Each receiver's home channel is divided into slots aligned to that
receiver's own grid, which its heartbeats tell every sender; a sender
with a frame for it waits for a slot boundary, checks the channel, and
transmits. No network time; no knowledge beyond the neighbour table.
Worked through in the order the pieces depend on each other.

**The phase.** A sender knows a receiver's grid only from the
receiver's heartbeats. The heartbeat's slot time is known from the ID,
but the heartbeat starts 0 to 872.8 ms after it, and in the loaded S4
run 31 % start more than 40 ms late and 18 % more than 120 ms: a slot
of a frame's length cannot be anchored on the heartbeat's start. The
heartbeat must carry its own phase, the time from its grid boundary to
its start: one byte at a 256th of a slot, 0.5 to 0.7 ms. A 14th byte
costs a block of symbols, 1.28 ms, 8.9 % of every heartbeat (spec
"Packet formats"), and is a format change. The ACK could carry the
receiver's phase too, to refresh it at every exchange, at 1.28 ms per
ACK, 7.5 %.

**The guard.** The sender's estimate ages until the next heartbeat it
hears from the receiver, once a rotation on the sender's channel, 65 to
195 s, twice that when one is lost (one in ten, roadmap D47): the
clocks part by up to 19.5 ms, or 39 ms. A transmission must start no
earlier than the receiver's boundary and end no later than the next
whatever the error, so the slot holds the frame, the check (3.1 ms),
the start (1.42 ms) and twice the drift:

| phase age | drift d | slot for the 129-byte text (74.6 ms) | for a full frame (138.6 ms) |
|---|---|---|---|
| one heartbeat, 15 s | 1.5 ms | 82 ms | 146 ms |
| a rotation, 195 s | 19.5 ms | 118 ms | 182 ms |
| two rotations, 390 s | 39 ms | 157 ms | 221 ms |

Frames differ in length, so a slot is sized for one length and a longer
frame takes two in a row. Nothing here changes with a shorter guard
than the clocks allow: a frame that starts before the boundary or
spills past the next collides with its neighbours' slots as if there
were none.

**What slots buy, among hidden pairs.** Two senders that cannot hear
each other and both address one receiver collide, unslotted, whenever
one starts within a frame's length before or after the other: a
vulnerable window of two frames, 2 T_f. Slotted at the receiver, they
collide only in the same slot: a window of one slot, L. Throughput of
pure against slotted random access is the classic pair of curves,
G e^(−2G) against G e^(−G), whose peaks stand at 18 % and 37 % of the
channel; the doubling is the halving of the window, and it holds only
when the slot is the frame. Here L is longer than T_f:

| phase age | L / 2 T_f, text | full frame |
|---|---|---|
| one heartbeat | 0.55 | 0.53 |
| a rotation | 0.79 | 0.66 |
| two rotations | 1.05 | 0.80 |

So with the phase a rotation old, slots remove a fifth of the hidden
pairs' collisions on text frames and a third on full frames; with the
phase refreshed by every ACK, nearly half; with a heartbeat lost,
nothing on text frames.

**What slots cost, among audible pairs.** Two senders that hear each
other almost never collide today: each checks the channel 3.1 ms
before it starts, and only a start inside that check and the 1.42 ms
after it, v ≈ 4.5 ms, is missed. The arrival times of their frames are
unrelated, so the chance that one falls in the other's 4.5 ms is small.
In slots, both wait for the same boundary, both check the quiet
channel in the same instant, and both transmit: a certain collision
whenever they choose the same slot. To keep what listen-before-talk
gives them, the slot must open with a contention window in which each
picks one of K check-lengths at random, and the one that picks earlier
is heard by the other: K × 4.5 ms added to L. Two audible senders in
one slot then collide one time in K; to make that as rare as today,
where the chance is about v / T_f, K must be about T_f / v ≈ 17, that
is 75 ms, and L for a text frame becomes 193 ms, 1.3 times 2 T_f. The
hidden pairs' gain is gone. With a smaller K the audible pairs pay what
the hidden pairs gain; the two windows trade against each other, and
their sum is what it was. Geometry says the two kinds are of the same
order: two senders placed at random within a receiver's range are
farther apart than that range 41 % of the time, and walls make more of
them hidden; the measured split of the same-receiver collisions is in
the table above.

**What slots do not reach.** Frames to *other* receivers on the same
channel follow those receivers' grids, which differ from this one's in
phase, so at this receiver they fall anywhere in its slots: their
collisions keep the unslotted window, neither worse nor better than
today. Heartbeats and beats keep their own schedules. The ACK goes on
the sender's channel at once, 17 ms long: slotted on the sender's grid
it would land in a slot where a DATA to the sender may also start, a
new collision, and it would wait up to a slot, which the 100 ms ACK
timeout would have to grow to cover; it stays as it is. Deliveries to
a sleepy node go in its window from its one holder, or with the long
preamble, which is three times a slot; no slots there either. So in the
dense city the slots reach 1.8 % of the DATA, and of that the hidden
pairs' part, and of that a fifth to a third: 0.4 to 0.6 points of
reception. Under load 8.3 %, and 2 to 3 points, where the bucket, not
the channel, is what binds (roadmap D93).

**Latency.** A frame ready now waits half a slot on average for the
boundary, 60 to 90 ms, on every attempt; and a first attempt cannot
simply take the next slot, since every sender to a relay that is
waiting for the same ban to end would take it too
(spec "Overload waits"): first attempts need the random spread over
several slots that retries have today, 180 ms or so per hop. The hand-over runs one at a
time, so the wait holds the queue. D97 found that idle time between
attempts acts as a brake on the storm; a slot wait would pace the
network by accident, and a measurement of slots would have to separate
that pacing from the alignment with an unaligned wait of the same
length as its control.

**A receiver whose phase is unknown.** One heard only in its burst,
whose heartbeats are in no slot (spec "Heartbeat"), or only from the
`aux` of a DATA, or speaking an older format, is sent to unslotted, as
today. It is the fallback and it is what every node does until the
format reaches it.

**Verdict.** Not worth building. The derivation bounds the gain at a
fifth to a third of the hidden part of a class that is 1.8 % of the
dense city's DATA, and shows that the audible pairs pay for it; the
measurement shows the losses are elsewhere.

## Candidate 2: scheduling by the busy estimate

The busy estimate is an exponential average over 30 minutes of a 0/1
reading of each channel every 13 s (spec "Busy estimate"): it says how
full a channel has been, which is what the move rule needs, and nothing
of when it is quiet. For a predictor at the frame scale the question is
whether the channel's occupancy has memory beyond the frame on the air,
which the check already sees.

From the dense city's observers, every relay's busy fraction of each
channel per second over the settled hour, the autocorrelation of a
channel's busy fraction at a lag of 1 s is 0.42 averaged over the
channels, 0.35 at 2 s, 0.18 at 5 s, 0.15 at 10 s, 0.09 at 30 s and
0.05 at 60 s, at a mean busy of 5.5 %. So the second-scale occupancy
has memory, the retries and the bans that cluster frames, but it fades
within seconds, and a predictor that said "this second is busier than
average" would hold a frame off a channel that is 90 % idle in that
second. At the 75 ms scale the occupants are text frames that arrive
at random and the retries their losses make, whose waits are random by
design (spec "Retry wait"); the only occupants known in advance are the
relays' heartbeats, whose slots every neighbour already knows, and
they cost 3.3 % of the dense city's DATA and 1.9 % under load. A sender
that kept its frames off the heartbeat slots of its receiver's
neighbours would need those neighbours in its own table, which holds
64 of the 340 in range. The texts are a Poisson process and the
occupancy is as memoryless as they are, bar the clustering that the
bans already answer.

**Verdict.** No predictor to build. The reading rate could not rise to
a useful one anyway: a reading costs 1.9 ms deaf, and the sweep's one
reading a second was chosen against that (roadmap D86).

## Candidate 3: spreading factor or channel offset as a second dimension

The SX1262 demodulates one spreading factor at a time
(spec "Physical layer", why the spreading factor is network-wide), so a
receiver listening at SF5 hears
nothing sent at another, and a second SF would split the network
rather than add a dimension. Channel offsets smaller than the 125 kHz
spacing are no dimension either: the signal is 125 kHz wide, and a
frame half a channel off is adjacent-channel interference after the
radio's partial rejection (open item O5), not a separate medium.

**Verdict.** Out; the chip has one demodulator.

## Candidate 4: anything that needs network time

A grid shared by every receiver on a channel would do what the
receiver-anchored one cannot: frames to other receivers would be
slotted too. Collisions with DATA to any receiver and with ACKs are
11.3 % of the dense city's DATA and 24 % under load, so the same
window ratios bound the gain at 2 to 4 points of reception in the
dense city and 5 to 8 under load, before the audible pairs' cost of
the contention window, which is the same here and eats the same share.

The time it needs is not there. A relay has GPS time only on external
power (roadmap D82) and only with a fix, which an indoor relay seldom
gets; a sleepy node never has it. A grid copied from a relay that has
it to one that does not, through the phase byte of Candidate 1, is a
clock-synchronisation protocol: every relay must choose which
neighbour's grid to follow and when to change, a reference rule with
its own failure modes, split references on one channel, a reference
that moves or sleeps, a district with none; and two districts without
a reference each settle on a grid of their own, so the scheme needs no
*global* time, only a locally agreed one, which is still time sync, and
still what the summary excludes. A relay-only plan with sleepy nodes
free-running would leave their beats, their own DATA and the
deliveries to them unslotted, and their own DATA is half of the DATA
the dense city's relays receive; the loaded S4 run's refused
and deaf receivers would stay as they are, since the bucket, not the
channel, refuses there.

**Verdict.** Out, as the summary says; and were it in, the gain is
bounded above by a few points before its own costs.

## How it was measured

`bark-sim` built from this branch, release, on main `8ff3a74`:
`dense-power.toml` seed 1 with `--json`, `--messages` and `--csv`; S4
at three times the rate (`scratchpad/storm1/s4-x3.toml`) seed 1;
`ring.toml` seed 1. The instrument is in `bark-sim/src/radio.rs`
(`how_of`, `note_addressed_loss`, `note_heartbeat_phase`) and
`metrics.rs` (`Addressed::lost_how`, `Addressed::heartbeat_late`); it
touches no random draw, and the JSON of the instrumented runs equals
main's on every field that existed before: the ring against a clean
build of `8ff3a74`, the dense city against another agent's run of the
same commit. The tables are read by `how.py` and the autocorrelation
by `acf.py`, both under the scratchpad's `slots/`. The heartbeat phase
is measured in the relay's own clock against the earliest phase seen
of its grid, so a few of the first heartbeats of a relay whose first
was late are counted later than they were; the bins are 40 ms. The
split of the same-receiver collisions into hidden and audible pairs
reads the link table of the start of the run, where links to a walker
are absent and count as hidden.

## What this does not show

- A built slot rule in the simulator. The bound derived here, with the
  measured share of the class it reaches, is below the spread between
  seeds that the storm notes report, so a prototype could not have
  measured its own effect; and its control, an unaligned wait of the
  same length, would have been a study of pacing, which is D97's
  subject.
- The board: the heartbeat's lateness and the clocks' drift are the
  simulator's, which takes the spec's ±50 ppm and the bench's radio
  costs (roadmap D86 to D88); the phase a real heartbeat would carry is
  not measured.
- The second dense-power seed; the verdict does not turn on it.

## Recommendation

Build none of the four. Receiver-anchored slots, the one candidate the
design could carry without network time, reach only the collisions of
two DATA to the same receiver, 1.8 % of the dense city's DATA and 8.3 %
under load, remove a fifth to a third of the hidden part of those once
the slot has taken its guard, and hand the rest back as collisions
among senders that hear each other, for a byte in every heartbeat and
a wait on every hop. What the measurement found instead is where the
frames go: a quarter of the dense city's DATA is lost to noise on links
whose receiver heard nothing else, and a sixth to receivers that were
transmitting, checking, elsewhere, or deaf from a frame they had just
lost. Those are the next questions: why the next hop's link has no
margin at the moment of the frame (spec "Neighbour table", its margin
rule; the 4 dB fade the simulator takes is roadmap D38's assumption, and a
sender's own RSSI of the receiver's heartbeats is on the sender's
channel, not the receiver's), and what a receiver that is deaf for its
own frames costs its senders. Neither is an access rule.
